\author{Author Name}

\begin{abstract}
Multislice simulation is the standard method for modeling electron beam-sample interactions in transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
The traditional multislice algorithm is calculated using a split-step Fourier method that employs a paraxial (Fresnel) propagator, limiting its accuracy
to small scattering angles (typically $<7^\circ$) and ignoring the change of the electron wavelength inside the sample potential.
These limitations can be significant in scenarios involving high-angle scattering or thick, high-Z samples.
In this work, we present a systematic comparison of three practical multislice propagators of increasing physical rigour:
Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, and the Wave Propagation Method (WPM) from the light optics community, benchmarked against a forward ODE solver for the Klein-Gordon equation.
We cast the Dirac, Klein-Gordon, and relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger equations into a common Helmholtz form, adapt the WPM to electron microscopy via a differentiable refractive-index binning strategy,
and benchmark the methods against high-angle reference solutions in two settings: the Rother--Scheerschmidt Au~[100] beam-amplitude verification and convergent-beam diffraction in Au~[110].
Our principal finding is that replacing the Fresnel propagation kernel with the exact angular spectrum kernel---a minimal, inexpensive modification to standard multislice---is
sufficient to capture high-angle scattering with accuracy comparable to the far more computationally expensive WPM and Klein-Gordon solvers in both tests.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction - Wave Equation}

Modern transmission electron microscopy (TEM) increasingly relies upon forward modeling of the electron--specimen interaction in order to interpret experimental data accurately. In 4D-STEM, electron ptychography, and related reconstruction methods, structural or chemical information is recovered by fitting a forward model of the electron--specimen interaction to measured images or diffraction patterns. The accuracy of quantities such as atomic positions, strain, or local composition is therefore limited not only by the data, but also by the physical approximations built into that forward model. The forward model we choose in this work is the Klein-Gordon equation, a relativistic, spinless wave equation that describes the evolution of a scalar wavefunction $\psi$ in the presence of an electrostatic potential $V(\mathbf{r})$. The Klein-Gordon equation can be neatly rearranged directly as a Helmholtz equation with an effective refractive index for an electron traversing an
electrostatic potential $V(\mathbf{r})$, which gives

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro}
\nabla^2 \psi + k_0^2\, n^2(\mathbf{r})\, \psi = 0,
\end{equation}

where $k_0 = \sqrt{E^2 - m_0^2 c^4}\,/\,\hbar c$ is the vacuum wavenumber and

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:n_intro}
n(\mathbf{r}) = \sqrt{\frac{\bigl(E - V(\mathbf{r})\bigr)^2 - m_0^2 c^4}{E^2 - m_0^2 c^4}}
\end{equation}

where $n(\mathbf{r})$ is an effective electron refractive index set by the local electrostatic potential. This expression equals unity in vacuum and departs from it only where the potential is nonzero, encoding the full relativistic energy-momentum relation of the electron in the presence of the specimen. The connection to the usual relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger equation is obtained by expanding the potential-dependent term in $k_0^2n^2$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linearized_index_intro}
k_0^2 n^2(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{\bigl(E-V(\mathbf r)\bigr)^2-m_0^2c^4}{\hbar^2c^2}
=
k_0^2-\frac{2EV(\mathbf r)}{\hbar^2c^2}+\frac{V^2(\mathbf r)}{\hbar^2c^2}.
\end{equation}
For $|V|\ll E$, the quadratic term can be neglected and, using $E=\gamma m_0c^2$, this becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro}
\nabla^2\psi+
\left(k_0^2-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2}V(\mathbf r)\right)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the refractive index has been linearised,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linearized_n_intro}
n(\mathbf r)\approx 1-\frac{\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2k_0^2}V(\mathbf r),
\end{equation}
up to the sign convention used for the electrostatic potential.

The standard route in electron microscopy is not to solve equation~\eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro} directly, 
but to approximate its forward solution by multislice. In multislice, the potential is treated as a sequence of thin projected slices: for a slice of thickness $\Delta z$, the projected potential $V_\mathrm{proj}(x,y)=\int_\mathrm{slice}V(x,y,z)\,dz$ gives the transmission factor $e^{i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj}}$, where $\sigma$ is the relativistic interaction constant. The wave is then propagated through the empty space between slices. Thus, once the thin-slice transmission approximation has been made, the central choice is the free-space propagator.

The first choice to use the exact free-space dispersion relation implied by equation~\eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro}. Setting $V=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nonparabolic_free_intro}
\left(\nabla_\perp^2+\partial_z^2+k_0^2\right)\psi = 0,
\end{equation}
where we have separated the full Laplacian into longitudinal and transverse parts,
\[
\nabla^2=\partial_z^2+\nabla_\perp^2,
\qquad
\nabla_\perp^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2,
\]
with $z$ the beam direction, $(x,y)$ the coordinates perpendicular to it, and $k_0$ the vacuum wavenumber. The free-space update to equation~\eqref{eq:nonparabolic_free_intro} is
the angular spectrum propagator, which applies the exact dispersion relation $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}$ to each transverse Fourier component,
\[
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
=
\mathcal F^{-1}\!\left[
\exp\!\left(i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\right],
\]
where $\mathcal F$ denotes the Fourier transform over the transverse coordinates. The square root is the exact free-space dispersion relation, so no small-angle or paraxial expansion has been made. Combining this free-space factor with the thin-slice transmission approximation yields angular spectrum multislice. It is not an exact solution of the full potential-dependent equation; it is a split-step method whose free-space step is non-paraxial.

The second choice is to make the small-angle, or high-energy, approximation before propagating. This replaces the exact square-root dispersion relation by its leading small-angle expansion,
\[
\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}
=k_0-\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}+\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{k_\perp^4}{k_0^3}\right).
\]
The first term, $k_0$, gives only the common carrier phase $e^{ik_0\Delta z}$. After removing this common phase, the remaining free-space propagation factor is the Fresnel multiplier
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fresnel_kernel_intro}
\exp\!\left(-i\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}\Delta z\right).
\end{equation}
Thus the Fresnel free-space update is
\[
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
=
\mathcal F^{-1}\!\left[
\exp\!\left(-i\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\right].
\]
The same approximation can also be written as the parabolic free-space Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:parabolic_free_intro}
\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{2k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\psi .
\end{equation}
Combining this parabolic free-space factor with the same thin-slice transmission approximation yields standard Fresnel multislice. The two multislice schemes therefore differ only in the free-space step:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:propagator_correspondence_intro}
\underbrace{\exp\!\left(i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,\Delta z\right)}_{\text{angular spectrum}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonparabolic_free_intro}},
\qquad
\underbrace{\exp\!\left(-i\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}\Delta z\right)}_{\text{Fresnel}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:parabolic_free_intro}}.
\end{equation}

Adding the same thin-slice potential term to the parabolic equation gives the familiar parabolic (paraxial) Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:paraxial_schrodinger}
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{2k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\psi + i\sigma V(x,y,z)\,\psi,
\end{equation}
which standard paraxial multislice typically solves. Angular spectrum multislice uses the same thin-slice transmission step, but replaces the Fresnel free-space step with the exact angular spectrum free-space step, in theory more accurately solving either \eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} or \eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro} depending on if the extra $V^2$ term is included in the potential evaluation. 

Also, as an alternative to multislice, the Bloch-wave method provides an efficient solution for thick, periodic crystals which contain no defects. Although the Bloch-wave and multislice methods are usually presented as distinct numerical formalisms, they commonly rely on the same high-energy reduction of the underlying wave equation. In both cases, the exact longitudinal dispersion is linearised about the incident beam direction, producing a one-way parabolic evolution equation. Consequently, the standard split-step Fresnel multislice method and the conventional Bloch-wave method are paraxial solutions of \eqref{eq:paraxial_schrodinger} which neglect high-angle scattering physics and do not fully retain the change of longitudinal wavelength inside the specimen potential.

Several authors in the field of electron microscopy have already examined how this approximation may be relaxed. Chen and Van Dyck derived an accurate multislice formulation directly from the Schrödinger equation without first making the usual high-energy approximation. After writing the wavefunction as a rapidly oscillating plane wave multiplied by a slowly varying envelope, the exact envelope equation still contains the second derivative in the beam direction. Retaining this second-order structure allows the equation to be factorised into forward- and backward-propagating branches. In the forward-scattering branch, the slice propagator contains an operator-valued square root whose free-space limit preserves the spherical dispersion relation
\[
k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_\perp^2},
\]
rather than replacing it by the Fresnel approximation
\[
k_z\simeq k-\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k}.
\]
This provides a natural non-paraxial reference formulation. Its computational cost, however, is much greater than conventional multislice because the transverse Laplacian and the projected crystal potential appear together inside the square-root operator. Since these operators do not commute, the propagator cannot be separated into the usual product of a Fourier-space Fresnel propagation factor and a real-space transmission function; instead, the square root must be approximated as an operator or matrix function. See Wacker and Schröder for a clear derivation of their method.

Rother and Scheerschmidt approached the same issue of how to include large angle scattering physics from a relativistic perspective. Starting from the Dirac equation, they compared Dirac, Klein--Gordon, and the usual scalar relativistically corrected Schrödinger equation, which they interpret as an approximated Klein--Gordon equation obtained by neglecting the \(V^2\) term. Their calculations show that, for typical TEM electrostatic and magnetic potentials, the differences between these relativistic descriptions are small. In contrast, the difference between numerical forward integration and the multislice approximation becomes significant for large-angle scattering. This suggests that the dominant high-angle error is not usually the neglect of spin, nor the use of a scalar relativistic equation, but the small-angle/parabolic propagation approximation used to obtain the standard multislice form.

Wacker and Schröder again explore how the inclusion of the second derivative affects the accuracy in low energy incident beams. After factoring out the incident plane wave, the exact envelope equation contains both \(\partial_z^2 A\) and \(2ik\partial_z A\). The high-energy approximation drops the second derivative, reducing the problem from a second-order equation in \(z\) to a one-way first-order evolution equation. This removes back-scattering and replaces the spherical Ewald-sphere dispersion by a parabolic one. They compare the conventional Fresnel multislice algorithm with Chen and Van Dyck's full multislice operator and with direct Runge--Kutta/Lobatto integrations of the non-approximated Schrödinger equation, showing that the high-energy approximation can produce substantial differences for low-voltage, thick-specimen, and high-angle scattering conditions.

Ultimately, the accurate solution of the non-paraxial problem wave equation is governed by a square-root longitudinal propagation operator, but evaluating this operator exactly is expensive when the specimen potential varies in the transverse direction. Many solutions of have been explored in the electron microscopy literature where several approximations to the same Helmholtz square-root structure have been developed, however the light and x-ray optics literature have also explored alternative practical approaches to the solution of this problem, which have not yet been made explicit in electron microscopy literature. In X-ray Ptychography, they already understand clearly that the angular spectrum method can improve accuracy by retaining high-angle propagation in free space, while leaving the specimen interaction in split-step form; Other methods such as Padé approximant methods replace the square root by rational approximations that remain accurate to larger angles than Fresnel  and are still somewhat computationally efficient; and Wave Propagation Method (WPM)-type schemes use a forward Helmholtz formulation that can include the local, potential-dependent wavelength inside the medium. In the next section, we implement and benchmark four models of increasing accuracy adopted from the light optics literature: standard Fresnel multislice, angular-spectrum multislice, and WPM-like high-angle propagation, comparing each against a direct numerical solution of the Klein--Gordon/Helmholtz equation for representative thick-sample and high-angle electron-scattering problems.


\section{Numerical methods}
\label{sec:methods}

We benchmark three practical multislice propagators for advancing the electron wave through the specimen, each
corresponding to a different level of approximation to the one-way Helmholtz
equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_intro}.\footnote{The full Dirac equation contributes a spin--orbit coupling
$-(i/\hbar c)(\vec\alpha\!\cdot\!\nabla V)\psi$ on the right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro};
its effect on beam amplitudes at the energies considered here is negligible \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009},
so we work throughout with the spinless Klein-Gordon equation.}
We describe them in order of decreasing physical rigour: a direct forward-ODE solver for the
full second-order Klein-Gordon equation, the Wave Propagation Method, the angular spectrum
multislice, and---for completeness---the standard Fresnel multislice derived above. The forward-ODE
solver makes no split-step or one-way approximation and serves throughout this paper as the
numerical ground truth against which the split-step schemes are benchmarked. In every scheme the
specimen is divided into thin slices of thickness $\Delta z$ perpendicular to the optic axis and the
wavefunction is advanced slice-by-slice on a transverse $N_x\times N_y$ grid; the methods differ
only in how the slice propagator is constructed.

\subsection{Forward Klein-Gordon ODE ground truth}
\label{sec:kg_ode}

The most accurate scheme retains the full second-order Klein-Gordon equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro}
without making the one-way approximation~\eqref{eq:one_way_intro}. Separating the Laplacian as
$\nabla^2 = \partial_z^2 + \nabla_\perp^2$, equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_second_order}
\partial_z^2 \psi \;=\; -\bigl(\nabla_\perp^2 + k_0^2\, n^2(x,y,z)\bigr)\,\psi,
\end{equation}
which we recast as a first-order system in the state $(\psi,\phi)$ with $\phi\equiv\partial_z\psi$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_state}
\frac{d}{dz}\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\\phi\end{pmatrix}
\;=\;
\begin{pmatrix} \phi \\ -\bigl(\nabla_\perp^2 + k_0^2 n^2(x,y,z)\bigr)\,\psi \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and integrate along $z$ with an adaptive high-order Runge--Kutta scheme (8th-order Dormand--Prince).
The right-hand side is evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ per stage by handling each term in its
diagonal basis: $\nabla_\perp^2\psi$ is computed via two FFTs (multiplication by $-k_\perp^2$ in
Fourier space) while $k_0^2 n^2(x,y,z)\,\psi$ is a pointwise real-space multiplication. The
potential is treated as piecewise constant over each slice of thickness $\Delta z$ and the step-size
controller is clipped at the slice boundaries so the solver never steps across a discontinuity in
$n^2(z)$. The wavefunction is initialised with the input probe $\psi(z=0)$ and the longitudinal
derivative
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_phi0}
\phi(z=0) \;=\; \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[i\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2}\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(z=0)\}\bigr],
\end{equation}
which enforces purely forward-propagating waves in the vacuum entrance plane.
This can be viewed as a real-space, arbitrary-probe generalisation of the forward-scattering ODE
benchmark used by Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, without the
periodic-crystal and beam-basis restrictions of that formulation. Because no split-step or paraxial factorisation is applied, the scheme reproduces the full
dispersion relation $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2 - k_\perp^2}$ and correctly couples the transverse
Laplacian to the spatially varying potential within each slice. Its cost is dominated by the FFT
pairs of the right-hand side evaluations; the adaptive controller refines the step inside the
specimen, so the ODE solver is typically several times more expensive than the split-step schemes
at matched accuracy, but no more so. We use it as the ground-truth reference throughout this work.

\subsection{Wave Propagation Method}
\label{sec:wpm}

The WPM, introduced by Brenner~\cite{Brenner1993} and developed into a systematic wave-optical
scheme for thick inhomogeneous elements by Schmidt et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016}, starts from the
exact slice propagator associated with the one-way wave equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_intro},
\[
\psi(z+\Delta z) \,=\, \exp\!\Bigl(i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2(x,y)+\nabla_\perp^2}\Bigr)\,\psi(z).
\]
For a homogeneous slice, $n$ is constant and therefore commutes with $\nabla_\perp^2$, so the
square-root operator is diagonal in Fourier space. Each transverse Fourier component then
propagates independently with longitudinal wavevector $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2-k_\perp^2}$, and the
one-slice update is obtained by multiplying the angular spectrum by $\exp(i k_z\Delta z)$.
Schmidt et al. derive in full in the appendix of their paper how this homogeneous-medium operator
can be extended to a thick inhomogeneous element by partitioning the transverse plane into
subdomains of nearly constant refractive index and applying the corresponding homogeneous-slice
propagator locally. The resulting approximation can be written in the continuum limit as a mixed
real/Fourier-space propagator,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint
\tilde\psi(k_x,k_y,z)\,e^{\,i\, k_z(x,y;k_\perp)\,\Delta z}\,
e^{i(k_x x + k_y y)}\,dk_x\,dk_y,
\end{equation}
with the spatially dependent longitudinal wavevector
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_kz}
k_z(x,y;k_\perp) \;=\; \sqrt{k_0^2\, n^2(x,y) - k_\perp^2}.
\end{equation}
In Schmidt's derivation, this is precisely how one goes beyond the thin-element approximation:
propagation and refraction are treated simultaneously through the local dispersion relation instead
of being factorised into a pure transmission function and a separate vacuum propagator. Unlike the
standard split-step, the WPM kernel depends \emph{simultaneously} on real-space
coordinates (through $n(x,y)$) and Fourier-space coordinates (through $k_\perp$), so the propagator
cannot be evaluated by a single FFT pair. The direct implementation costs $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ per
slice, which is prohibitive at typical TEM grid sizes. Following \cite{Schmidt2016,DiffWPM2025} we
sample $n(x,y)$ by a small set of reference values $\{n_m\}_{m=1}^{N_\mathrm{bin}}$ so that the WPM
step decomposes into a bank of homogeneous-slice angular spectrum propagations followed by a
spatially weighted recombination,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bank}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;\approx\; \sum_{m=1}^{N_\mathrm{bin}} w_m(x,y)\;
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[e^{\,i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2 n_m^2 - k_\perp^2}}\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(x,y,z)\}\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $w_m(x,y)$ interpolates the local refractive index between adjacent bins. This reduces the
per-slice cost to $\mathcal{O}(N_\mathrm{bin}\,N\log N)$. We use the differentiable binning scheme
described in Appendix~\ref{sec:binning}, which selects $\{n_m\}$ on a quadratic grid in
$[n_\mathrm{min},n_\mathrm{max}]$ and uses a smoothstep weight $w_m$ so that the propagator remains
differentiable with respect to the potential. When $n(x,y)$ is constant across the transverse plane
a single bin suffices and the WPM reduces exactly to the angular spectrum method described next.

\subsection{Angular spectrum multislice}
\label{sec:asm}

The angular spectrum method (ASM) replaces the paraxial Fresnel kernel of
equation~\eqref{eq:ms_intro} with the exact free-space dispersion relation while retaining the
split-step structure of standard multislice. The homogeneous-slice propagator in Fourier space is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_kernel}
\hat P_\mathrm{ASM}(k_\perp;\Delta z) \;=\; \exp\!\bigl(i\Delta z\,\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the one-slice update is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_step}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;\approx\; e^{\,i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj}(x,y)}\;
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[\hat P_\mathrm{ASM}(k_\perp;\Delta z)\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(x,y,z)\}\bigr].
\end{equation}
The transmission factor is identical to standard multislice. Replacing only the Fresnel kernel
costs nothing: the FFT pair is unchanged and the per-slice cost remains $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$. The
relationship to Fresnel multislice is made transparent by expanding the square-root operator,
\begin{equation*}
\sqrt{k_0^2 + \nabla_\perp^2} \;=\; k_0 + \frac{\nabla_\perp^2}{2k_0}
- \frac{\nabla_\perp^4}{8k_0^3} + \cdots,
\end{equation*}
where truncating at the second term recovers the Fresnel kernel
$\exp(-ik_\perp^2\Delta z/(2k_0))$, whereas ASM retains the operator to all orders. ASM is the
minimal correction to standard multislice that captures the correct free-space high-angle
propagation while preserving its split-step structure and FFT efficiency. It differs from the WPM
only in that, inside the potential, the wavelength is held at its vacuum value $\lambda_0=2\pi/k_0$
rather than tracking the local $\lambda(x,y)=2\pi/(k_0 n(x,y))$.

\subsection{Fresnel multislice}
\label{sec:fms}

For completeness we recall the standard split-step Fresnel multislice scheme derived in the
Introduction (equation~\eqref{eq:ms_intro}): the slice propagator combines a transmission factor
$\exp(i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj})$ with the paraxial Fresnel kernel
$\exp(-ik_\perp^2\Delta z/(2k_0))$, obtained by truncating the square-root operator at second order
in $k_\perp/k_0$. It is the cheapest of these schemes---one FFT pair per slice---and the
standard tool of quantitative TEM simulation. Its accuracy is bounded by the paraxial assumption
$k_\perp^2 \ll k_0^2$.

\section{Results}

We present two numerical experiments that isolate the high-angle behaviour most relevant to this
work. Both compare Fresnel multislice (FMS), angular spectrum multislice (ASMS), and the Wave
Propagation Method (WPM) against a converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon
equation. The first reproduces the Au~[100] beam-amplitude benchmark of Rother and
Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}; the second tests a convergent Au~[110] probe over a
wide range of semi-angles.

\subsection{Verification against Rother \& Scheerschmidt (2009)}
\label{sec:verification}

To verify the propagation methods, we recreated the simulation presented in figure~3 of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009},
which tracks the amplitude of a central beam $[0,0]$ and a high-angle beam $[0,28]$ at 135~mrad
after propagation through a thick gold [100] crystal at 300~keV.
The ground truth is a converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon equation.

For the central beam, all methods agree closely.
For the high-angle beam at 135~mrad, the Fresnel multislice solution diverges from the ground truth after approximately five unit cells,
consistent with the findings of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009} who attributed the discrepancy to the linearization of the square-root operator.
However, the angular spectrum multislice solution---which differs from Fresnel MS only by replacing the parabolic propagation kernel
$\exp(-i k_\perp^2 \Delta z / 2k_0)$ with the exact kernel $\exp(i\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2}\,\Delta z)$---closely tracks the more expensive
WPM and KG~ODE solutions. This is the first indication that the angular spectrum kernel alone captures the essential high-angle physics.
Table~\ref{tab:rmse_verification} quantifies this observation with RMSE values computed over the first 25 unit cells against the digitized paper reference.

\subsection{Convergent-probe Au CBED}
\label{sec:convergence_angle}

Next, we test each method's accuracy as a function of probe convergence semi-angle for a convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) geometry.
A convergent probe with semi-angles ranging from 5 to 100~mrad is propagated through the same Au~[110] crystal at 200~keV,
using 32 slices per unit cell for the test methods and a KG~ODE ground truth with 128 slices per unit cell.

The relative $L^2$ diffraction-pattern error is then evaluated as a function of semi-angle.
Across the tested angular range, the angular spectrum method provides accuracy comparable to WPM,
while the Fresnel propagator shows a growing error at higher convergence angles.
This confirms in a realistic probe geometry that the dominant correction is the replacement of the
paraxial Fresnel kernel by the exact angular-spectrum kernel; the additional local-index machinery
of WPM produces only small changes for this tested Au geometry.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a focused comparison of Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, and the Wave
Propagation Method, benchmarked against a forward ODE solution of the full second-order
Klein-Gordon equation. The comparison was restricted to two high-angle tests: the Au~[100]
beam-amplitude verification against Rother and Scheerschmidt and convergent-beam diffraction in
Au~[110] at probe semi-angles up to 100~mrad.

Our principal finding is that replacing the Fresnel propagation kernel with the exact angular spectrum kernel---a minimal
and inexpensive modification to standard multislice---is sufficient to capture high-angle scattering with accuracy
comparable to the far more computationally expensive WPM and Klein-Gordon solvers.
Within these tests, the WPM does not provide a clear accuracy advantage over angular spectrum
multislice, suggesting that the dominant missing physics in standard multislice is the
non-paraxial free-space dispersion rather than the local refractive-index dependence of the
propagation kernel.

These results do not diminish the theoretical value of the WPM or full Klein-Gordon solvers:
they remain essential as ground-truth references and may prove necessary in extreme regimes not tested here
(e.g., very thick high-Z samples at energies below 100~keV).
However, for the broad range of conditions encountered in modern TEM---including convergent-beam
simulations---the angular spectrum multislice method provides an excellent balance
of accuracy and computational efficiency.

The differentiable refractive-index binning strategy introduced for the WPM remains a useful contribution,
as it enables gradient-based inverse problems such as ptychography to be formulated with a wide-angle
forward model when needed. Future work will explore the limits of the angular spectrum approximation
in extreme scattering geometries and integrate the method with large-scale ptychographic reconstructions.

\section{Differentiable refractive-index binning for WPM multislice}
\label{sec:binning}

To make the computation of the wave propagation method tractable and limit the need for $O(N^{2D})$ computations, we adapt the WPM algorithm presented in \cite{DiffWPM2025} which
uses a refractive index binning strategy to limit the number of unique refractive index values in a slice while maintaining differentiability with respect to the refractive index map $n(x,y)$.

To implement this method, we first discretize the refractive index range $[n_{\mathrm{min}}, n_{\mathrm{max}}]$ of the current slice into $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ discrete reference values, denoted as
$\{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}}$.

In a typical multislice potential, many of the refractive index values will be very close to $1$ (vacuum region), while the refractive index values of an atom
will concentrate in a narrow range slightly above $1$. Since there are likely to be many more refractive index values near the vacuum region,
we employ a quadratic distribution for the reference indices rather than using linearly spaced bins in order to allocate more bins near $n=1$.
The reference indices are distributed according to a quadratic function determined by the parameter $\gamma$, with $\gamma=2.0$ for quadratic spacing:

\[
n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)} = n_{\mathrm{min}} + (n_{\mathrm{max}} - n_{\mathrm{min}}) \left( \frac{k}{N_{\mathrm{bins}}-1} \right)^\gamma
\]

which allows for denser binning near the vacuum refractive index when $\gamma > 1$.

Next, for a propagation step $\Delta z$, we compute a bank of propagated fields $u_k(x,y)$. Each field $u_k$ corresponds to the
diffraction of the input field $u_{\mathrm{in}}(x,y)$ assuming a homogeneous medium with refractive index $n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}$.
This is performed efficiently via batch FFT operations using the angular spectrum kernel:

\[
u_k(x,y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left( \mathcal{F}(u_{\mathrm{in}}) e^{i \Delta z \sqrt{(k_0 n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)})^2 - k_x^2 - k_y^2}} \right)
\]

To evaluate the field at a specific spatial coordinate $(x,y)$ where the local refractive index is $n(x,y)$,
we identify the two bounding reference bins such that $n_L \le n(x,y) < n_R$, where $n_L, n_R \in \{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}$.
We adopt a differentiable interpolation scheme by defining a local interpolation weight

\[
w_{\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{n(x,y) - n_L}{n_R - n_L}, \quad w_{\mathrm{raw}} \in [0, 1]
\]

To ensure smooth gradients and minimize discretization artifacts, we map this linear weight through a smoothstep activation function $S(w)$,
akin to the spatial partitioning technique demonstrated by \cite{BarreBrunel} via a cubic Hermite interpolation function:

\[
w = S(w_{\mathrm{raw}}) = 3w_{\mathrm{raw}}^2 - 2w_{\mathrm{raw}}^3
\]
While linear interpolation would satisfy the requirement for differentiability, it could result in discontinuities in the gradient at the bin boundaries in a gradient-based optimization scheme.

The final field at the next step $u(x,y, z+\Delta z)$ is computed as the weighted superposition of the fields
propagated at the bounding indices:

\[
u(x,y) = (1 - w) \cdot u_L(x,y) + w \cdot u_R(x,y)
\]

This formulation ensures that the propagation model is fully differentiable with respect to the refractive index map $n(x,y)$, enabling inverse reconstruction of potentials via automatic differentiation,
and that only a limited number of FFTs (equal to $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$) are required per propagation step, significantly reducing the computational cost compared to a naive WPM implementation.
The accuracy of this binned WPM multislice method depends on the number of bins $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ chosen and will change on a case-by-case basis depending on the refractive index distribution of the sample.
One can mitigate this potential source of error by performing a test simulation and doubling the number of bins until no difference is observed in the output wavefunction.

In general, the WPM will need a GPU for efficient computation, and we have found that $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ between 64 and 512 are sufficient for the simulations presented in this work.
This is a marked improvement over the naive case, as it enables the WPM to simulate many slices with larger grid sizes ($512 \times 512$ or $1024 \times 1024$ pixels)
in a reasonable time frame (seconds to minutes), provided the refractive index binning strategy is capable of correctly capturing the refractive index distribution of the sample.
