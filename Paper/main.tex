\title{Wide-Angle Multislice for Electron Microscopy via the Wave Propagation Method}
\author{Author Name}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Multislice simulation is the standard method for modeling electron beam-sample interactions in transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
The traditional multislice algorithm is calculated using a split-step Fourier method that employs a paraxial (Fresnel) propagator, limiting its accuracy
to small scattering angles (typically $<7^\circ$) and ignoring the change of the electron wavelength inside the sample potential.
These limitations can be significant in scenarios involving high-angle scattering or thick, high-Z samples.
In this work, we present a comparison of three practical multislice propagators of increasing physical complexity:
Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, and the Wave Propagation Method (WPM) from the light optics community, benchmarked against a forward ODE solver for the Klein-Gordon equation.
We write  Klein-Gordon, in it's Helmholtz form, adapt the WPM to electron microscopy via a differentiable refractive-index binning strategy,
and benchmark the methods against high-angle reference solutions in two settings: the Rother--Scheerschmidt Au~[100] beam-amplitude verification and convergent-beam diffraction in Au~[110].
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}


Transmission and scanning transmission electron microscopy are among the primary experimental tools for characterising materials at the atomic scale. Over the past two decades, successive generations of aberration correctors have reduced the dominant probe-forming aberrations, convergence semi-angles have grown from a few milliradians to 30--80~mrad, and lateral resolution has approached the sub-\r{A}ngstr\"{o}m regime. In parallel, direct electron detectors---which detect individual electrons by direct conversion---now combine frame rates of several kilohertz with detective quantum efficiencies substantially higher than conventional charge-coupled devices. Together, these developments allow a statistically useful diffraction pattern to be recorded at every probe position during a STEM raster without prohibitive acquisition times, producing four-dimensional datasets that encode the angular distribution of scattered electrons across an extended field of view.

These 4D-STEM datasets and the computational tools used to understand them underpin a growing range of quantitative measurement methods: ptychographic phase reconstruction, electric and magnetic field mapping, strain analysis, and three-dimensional structure determination. In several of these methods, multislice simulation is used not only to interpret results after the fact but as the physical forward model inside iterative reconstruction pipelines, where simulated diffraction intensities are compared directly against experimental measurements during optimisation. Yet despite the rapid development of electron-optical hardware and detector technology, the propagation algorithm at the core of these simulations---the multislice method, introduced by Cowley and Moodie in the 1950s---has remained largely unchanged. The feasibility of using multislice at scale has been aided by the widespread availability of GPU computing and automatic differentiation frameworks, which have made differentiating through large multislice computations practical. As instruments continue to improve, as detectors collect more high-angle scattering information per experiment, and as reconstruction methods attempt to become more quantitative, the accuracy of the forward model becomes correspondingly more important. Systematic errors in the propagator accumulate over many probe positions, scattering angles, and propagation steps, and can bias reconstructed quantities in ways that are not obvious from the residuals alone.

The standard forward model for electron propagation through a crystalline specimen is multislice, primarily because of its ease of implementation, computational efficiency, and FFT-based split-step structure. In multislice, the specimen is divided into thin slices perpendicular to the beam; each slice applies a projected transmission function that phase-shifts the wave, after which the wave is propagated through free space. In its conventional implementation this free-space step uses the paraxial Fresnel propagator, obtained by replacing the exact spherical free-space phase factor $\exp(i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,\Delta z)$ with a parabolic approximation valid when transverse momenta remain small compared to the forward momentum. For the low convergence angles and thin specimens still typical of much quantitative TEM and STEM work, this approximation is adequate, and the dominant sources of discrepancy are thermal diffuse scattering, detector response, and aberration characterisation rather than the propagator itself. At low electron energies, large convergence angles, and in thicker specimens with high-$Z$ materials, the paraxial assumption becomes a more significant source of error.

Corrections to the paraxial propagation step have been explored within electron microscopy, but methods that fully preserve large-angle scattering generally introduce operator-valued square roots that cannot be evaluated by the single FFT pair used in standard multislice. The central question is therefore whether high-angle accuracy can be recovered without giving up the computational structure that made multislice useful: a real-space transmission step combined with a Fourier-domain propagation kernel. The mathematical origin of this question is easiest to see by writing the relativistic wave equation and comparing the exact free-space propagator with its Fresnel approximation.

We begin from the equation most widely used to describe elastic electron--specimen interaction in TEM: the time-independent Schr\"odinger equation with a relativistic correction,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro}
\nabla^2\psi+
\left(k_0^2-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2}V(\mathbf r)\right)\psi=0,
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ is the electron wavefunction, $V(\mathbf r)$ the local electrostatic potential energy, $m_0$ the electron rest mass, $\hbar$ the reduced Planck constant, $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ the relativistic Lorentz factor, $c$ the speed of light, and $k_0 = \sqrt{E^2 - m_0^2 c^4}\,/\,\hbar c$ the vacuum wavenumber set by the total relativistic energy $E=\gamma m_0 c^2$.

This Schr\"odinger equation can also be written as a Helmholtz wave equation by grouping the two terms multiplying $\psi$ into a single refractive-index factor. This is useful because the possible approximations then appear as changes to $n(\mathbf r)$, rather than as separate-looking wave equations. Dividing the coefficient in equation~\eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro} by the vacuum factor $k_0^2$ defines the relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger index,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:index_schrodinger}
n_\mathrm{S}^2(\mathbf r)
\equiv
\frac{1}{k_0^2}\left(k_0^2-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2}V(\mathbf r)\right)
=
1-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2 k_0^2}\,V(\mathbf r).
\end{equation}
The same scalar wave equation can then be written in the compact Helmholtz form,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro}
\nabla^2 \psi + k_0^2\, n^2(\mathbf{r})\, \psi = 0.
\end{equation}
The index equals unity in vacuum and departs from it wherever the potential is nonzero. Its form is the first approximation choice: starting from the Schr\"odinger index~\eqref{eq:index_schrodinger}, adding or removing terms selects the underlying wave equation while preserving the same Helmholtz notation,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:n_intro}
\begin{align}
n_\mathrm{KG}^2(\mathbf r)
&= n_\mathrm{S}^2(\mathbf r)+\frac{V^2(\mathbf r)}{\hbar^2 c^2 k_0^2}
= \frac{\bigl(E - V(\mathbf r)\bigr)^2 - m_0^2 c^4}{E^2 - m_0^2 c^4}, \label{eq:index_kg}\\[6pt]
n_\mathrm{NR}^2(\mathbf r)
&= 1 - \frac{2m_0}{\hbar^2 k_\mathrm{NR}^2}\,V(\mathbf r),
\qquad k_\mathrm{NR}=\frac{\sqrt{2m_0 T}}{\hbar}. \label{eq:index_nonrel}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Equation~\eqref{eq:index_kg} adds back the quadratic potential-energy term that appears in the full relativistic energy--momentum relation $(E-V)^2=m_0^2c^4+\hbar^2c^2k^2$; substituting this index into equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} gives the time-independent Klein--Gordon equation, which is used below as the ground-truth reference. Equation~\eqref{eq:index_nonrel} instead removes the relativistic mass and dispersion corrections by replacing $\gamma m_0$ with $m_0$ and $k_0$ with the non-relativistic vacuum wavenumber $k_\mathrm{NR}$ for kinetic energy $T$. Thus the first line of approximation is the choice of refractive index: full relativistic Klein--Gordon, relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger, or non-relativistic Schr\"odinger.

After the refractive index has been chosen, a second and independent approximation controls the propagation direction. Splitting the Laplacian as $\nabla^2=\partial_z^2+\nabla_\perp^2$ and factoring out the rapidly varying carrier wave, $\psi=e^{ik_0z}\tilde\psi$, rewrites the Helmholtz equation as an envelope equation containing both $\partial_z\tilde\psi$ and $\partial_z^2\tilde\psi$. The high-energy, one-way, or paraxial approximation assumes the envelope varies slowly along $z$ and drops $\partial_z^2\tilde\psi$, giving the parabolic Helmholtz equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:paraxial_schrodinger}
\frac{\partial\tilde\psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{2k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\tilde\psi
+\frac{i k_0}{2}\left[n^2(x,y,z)-1\right]\tilde\psi.
\end{equation}
This is the paraxial Helmholtz form of the same refractive-index model.

If the multislice algorithm is employed to solve either the second-order Helmholtz equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} or the paraxial Helmholtz equation~\eqref{eq:paraxial_schrodinger}, the difference appears in the free-space propagator inserted between thin specimen slices. Setting $V=0$ in the second-order equation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nonparabolic_free_intro}
\left(\nabla_\perp^2+\partial_z^2+k_0^2\right)\psi = 0,
\end{equation}
where $z$ is the beam direction and $(x,y)$ are transverse coordinates. Keeping equation~\eqref{eq:nonparabolic_free_intro} in this second-order form gives the angular spectrum propagator, which applies the exact spherical dispersion relation $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}$ to each transverse Fourier component. Applying the paraxial approximation instead is equivalent in free space to expanding the square root,
\[
\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}=k_0-\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}+\mathcal O\!\left(\frac{k_\perp^4}{k_0^3}\right),
\]
and absorbing the carrier phase $e^{ik_0\Delta z}$, which gives the Fresnel propagator. The two free-space kernels therefore differ only in how they treat longitudinal dispersion:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:propagator_correspondence_intro}
\underbrace{\exp\!\left(i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,\Delta z\right)}_{\text{angular spectrum: exact dispersion}}
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\underbrace{\exp\!\left(-i\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}\Delta z\right)}_{\text{Fresnel: parabolic dispersion}}.
\end{equation}
Thus a multislice discretisation of the second-order Helmholtz equation leads naturally to angular spectrum multislice, while a multislice discretisation of the paraxial Helmholtz equation leads to Fresnel multislice. The Bloch-wave formulation traditionally applies the same parabolic linearisation about the incident beam direction, so conventional Bloch-wave and Fresnel multislice are two numerical realisations of the same one-way paraxial approximation. This is accurate for small scattering angles and thin specimens, but it breaks down at high angles and in thick, high-$Z$ materials where the neglected longitudinal curvature becomes significant.

The central difficulty is that the split-step form avoids the true medium-propagation operator. It applies the specimen as a thin transmission function and then propagates the wave through vacuum. If one wants to propagate through the medium itself, or to retain backward-propagating components, the starting point is again the second-order Helmholtz equation, now written as an evolution problem in $z$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:helmholtz_operator_intro}
\partial_z^2\psi
=
-\mathcal{H}_\perp(z)\psi,
\qquad
\mathcal{H}_\perp(z)
=
\nabla_\perp^2+k_0^2n^2(x,y,z).
\end{equation}
For a homogeneous slice, $n$ is constant and this operator is diagonal in Fourier space, giving $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2n^2-k_\perp^2}$. In a real specimen, however, $n(x,y,z)$ varies across the slice. The operator then contains both transverse differentiation and multiplication by $n^2(x,y,z)$, so it is not diagonal in either real space or Fourier space.

There are two ways to proceed from equation~\eqref{eq:helmholtz_operator_intro}. If backward waves are retained, the second-order equation is solved as a two-component first-order system,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:second_order_state_intro}
\frac{d}{dz}
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi\\
\partial_z\psi
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1\\
-\mathcal{H}_\perp(z) & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi\\
\partial_z\psi
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This is the most direct route when backscattered components are to be included. If instead a one-way forward equation is desired, the same second-order operator is formally factorised and the forward branch is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}
\partial_z\psi
=
i\sqrt{\mathcal{H}_\perp(z)}\,\psi
=
i\sqrt{k_0^2n^2(x,y,z)+\nabla_\perp^2}\,\psi.
\end{equation}
This square-root operator is the object that must be approximated if one wants high-angle one-way propagation without falling back to a purely vacuum propagator.

The cost of this operator is what has been examined repeatedly in the electron microscopy literature. Chen and Van Dyck derived an accurate multislice scheme directly from the Schr\"odinger equation without making the high-energy approximation: they factor out the carrier wave but retain the second derivative in $z$, so the envelope equation separates into forward- and backward-propagating branches. The forward branch contains an operator-valued square root of the same kind as equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}. Because this square root mixes real-space multiplication by the specimen potential with transverse differentiation, it cannot be evaluated as the usual product of a Fourier-space propagator and a real-space transmission function. It must instead be approximated as an operator or matrix function, at substantially higher cost than conventional multislice. Wacker and Schröder give a clear derivation of this construction and benchmark it against conventional Fresnel multislice and direct Runge--Kutta/Lobatto integration of the non-approximated Schr\"odinger equation, showing that dropping the second $z$-derivative introduces substantial errors at low voltages, large thicknesses, and high scattering angles. Rother and Scheerschmidt approached this problem from a relativistic perspective: their comparison of Dirac, Klein--Gordon, and relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger calculations confirms that the dominant high-angle error is the parabolic propagation step of the Fresnel multislice method itself. In the electron-microscopy literature, high-angle-accurate treatments have therefore tended to appear as direct integrations, matrix methods, or operator approximations rather than as drop-in replacements for the Fourier-domain propagator in standard multislice.

The light and X-ray optics communities have already explored solutions to including high angle effects without solving an expensive ODE. The simplest correction is to keep the specimen interaction in split-step form but replace the Fresnel free-space kernel with the angular spectrum kernel, which is exact when $n=1$ and already recovers the spherical free-space dispersion. Other solutions, known as Padé approximant methods approximate the square root in equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_square_root_intro} by rational functions, extending the angular range while preserving a split-step structure. In light optics, the Wave Propagation Method (WPM) can be understood as another practical approximation to equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}: within each slice, WPM treats small transverse regions as locally homogeneous, applies the homogeneous one-way propagator $\exp\!\left(i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2n_m^2-k_\perp^2}\right)$ for representative refractive indices $n_m$, and recombines those propagated fields according to the local value of $n(x,y)$. It therefore keeps the potential-dependent wavelength inside the propagation step without forming the full dense square-root operator directly. The WPM also permits a backscattering solution that can still avail of the Fourier Transform. These techniques span a useful accuracy--cost trade-off that have been systematically benchmarked in the electron-microscopy regime. The aim of this work is to evaluate that trade-off for electron-microscopy specimens: can high-angle propagation be recovered while retaining the FFT-based split-step structure that makes multislice practical?


\section{Numerical methods}
\label{sec:methods}

We compare four models of increasing physical complexity---standard Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, the Wave Propagation Method, and a direct forward-ODE solver for the full second-order Klein--Gordon equation obtained from the Helmholtz form~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} with the full relativistic index~\eqref{eq:index_kg}. The three approximate solvers are benchmarked against the Klein--Gordon ODE reference in two high-angle settings: the Au~[100] beam-amplitude benchmark of Rother and Scheerschmidt, which tracks a 135~mrad beam through a thick gold crystal, and a convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) geometry in Au~[110], where we test each method as a function of probe convergence semi-angle up to 100~mrad. In every case the specimen is divided into thin slices of thickness $\Delta z$ perpendicular to the optic axis and the wavefunction is advanced slice-by-slice on a transverse $N_x\times N_y$ grid; the methods differ only in how the slice propagator is constructed. The forward-ODE solver makes no split-step or one-way approximation and serves as the numerical ground truth throughout.

\subsection{Forward Klein-Gordon ODE ground truth}
\label{sec:kg_ode}

The most accurate scheme retains the full second-order Klein-Gordon equation obtained by using the full relativistic index~\eqref{eq:index_kg} in the Helmholtz form~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro}, without making a one-way approximation. Separating the Laplacian as
$\nabla^2 = \partial_z^2 + \nabla_\perp^2$, equation~\eqref{eq:kg_helmholtz_intro} reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_second_order}
\partial_z^2 \psi \;=\; -\bigl(\nabla_\perp^2 + k_0^2\, n^2(x,y,z)\bigr)\,\psi,
\end{equation}
which we recast as a first-order system in the state $(\psi,\phi)$ with $\phi\equiv\partial_z\psi$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_state}
\frac{d}{dz}\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\\phi\end{pmatrix}
\;=\;
\begin{pmatrix} \phi \\ -\bigl(\nabla_\perp^2 + k_0^2 n^2(x,y,z)\bigr)\,\psi \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and integrate along $z$ with an adaptive high-order Runge--Kutta scheme (8th-order Dormand--Prince).
The right-hand side is evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ per stage by handling each term in its
diagonal basis: $\nabla_\perp^2\psi$ is computed via two FFTs (multiplication by $-k_\perp^2$ in
Fourier space) while $k_0^2 n^2(x,y,z)\,\psi$ is a pointwise real-space multiplication. The
potential is treated as piecewise constant over each slice of thickness $\Delta z$ and the step-size
controller is clipped at the slice boundaries so the solver never steps across a discontinuity in
$n^2(z)$. The wavefunction is initialised with the input probe $\psi(z=0)$ and the longitudinal
derivative
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_phi0}
\phi(z=0) \;=\; \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[i\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2}\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(z=0)\}\bigr],
\end{equation}
which enforces purely forward-propagating waves in the vacuum entrance plane.
This can be viewed as a real-space, arbitrary-probe generalisation of the forward-scattering ODE
benchmark used by Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, without the
periodic-crystal and beam-basis restrictions of that formulation. Because no split-step or paraxial factorisation is applied, the scheme reproduces the full
dispersion relation $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2 - k_\perp^2}$ and correctly couples the transverse
Laplacian to the spatially varying potential within each slice. Its cost is dominated by the FFT
pairs of the right-hand side evaluations; the adaptive controller refines the step inside the
specimen, so the ODE solver is typically several times more expensive than the split-step schemes
at matched accuracy, but no more so. We use it as the ground-truth reference throughout this work.

\subsection{Wave Propagation Method}
\label{sec:wpm}

The WPM, introduced by Brenner~\cite{Brenner1993} and developed into a systematic wave-optical
scheme for thick inhomogeneous elements by Schmidt et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016}, starts from the
exact slice propagator associated with the corresponding one-way wave equation,
\[
\psi(z+\Delta z) \,=\, \exp\!\Bigl(i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2(x,y)+\nabla_\perp^2}\Bigr)\,\psi(z).
\]
For a homogeneous slice, $n$ is constant and therefore commutes with $\nabla_\perp^2$, so the
square-root operator is diagonal in Fourier space. Each transverse Fourier component then
propagates independently with longitudinal wavevector $k_z=\sqrt{k_0^2 n^2-k_\perp^2}$, and the
one-slice update is obtained by multiplying the angular spectrum by $\exp(i k_z\Delta z)$.
Schmidt et al. derive in full in the appendix of their paper how this homogeneous-medium operator
can be extended to a thick inhomogeneous element by partitioning the transverse plane into
subdomains of nearly constant refractive index and applying the corresponding homogeneous-slice
propagator locally. The resulting approximation can be written in the continuum limit as a mixed
real/Fourier-space propagator,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint
\tilde\psi(k_x,k_y,z)\,e^{\,i\, k_z(x,y;k_\perp)\,\Delta z}\,
e^{i(k_x x + k_y y)}\,dk_x\,dk_y,
\end{equation}
with the spatially dependent longitudinal wavevector
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_kz}
k_z(x,y;k_\perp) \;=\; \sqrt{k_0^2\, n^2(x,y) - k_\perp^2}.
\end{equation}
In Schmidt's derivation, this is precisely how one goes beyond the thin-element approximation:
propagation and refraction are treated simultaneously through the local dispersion relation instead
of being factorised into a pure transmission function and a separate vacuum propagator. Unlike the
standard split-step, the WPM kernel depends \emph{simultaneously} on real-space
coordinates (through $n(x,y)$) and Fourier-space coordinates (through $k_\perp$), so the propagator
cannot be evaluated by a single FFT pair. The direct implementation costs $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ per
slice, which is prohibitive at typical TEM grid sizes. Following \cite{Schmidt2016,DiffWPM2025} we
sample $n(x,y)$ by a small set of reference values $\{n_m\}_{m=1}^{N_\mathrm{bin}}$ so that the WPM
step decomposes into a bank of homogeneous-slice angular spectrum propagations followed by a
spatially weighted recombination,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bank}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;\approx\; \sum_{m=1}^{N_\mathrm{bin}} w_m(x,y)\;
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[e^{\,i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2 n_m^2 - k_\perp^2}}\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(x,y,z)\}\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $w_m(x,y)$ interpolates the local refractive index between adjacent bins. This reduces the
per-slice cost to $\mathcal{O}(N_\mathrm{bin}\,N\log N)$. We use the differentiable binning scheme
described in Appendix~\ref{sec:binning}, which selects $\{n_m\}$ on a quadratic grid in
$[n_\mathrm{min},n_\mathrm{max}]$ and uses a smoothstep weight $w_m$ so that the propagator remains
differentiable with respect to the potential. When $n(x,y)$ is constant across the transverse plane
a single bin suffices and the WPM reduces exactly to the angular spectrum method described next.

\subsection{Angular spectrum multislice}
\label{sec:asm}

The angular spectrum method (ASM) replaces the paraxial Fresnel kernel of
equation~\eqref{eq:propagator_correspondence_intro} with the exact free-space dispersion relation while retaining the
split-step structure of standard multislice. The homogeneous-slice propagator in Fourier space is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_kernel}
\hat P_\mathrm{ASM}(k_\perp;\Delta z) \;=\; \exp\!\bigl(i\Delta z\,\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the one-slice update is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_step}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z) \;\approx\; e^{\,i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj}(x,y)}\;
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\bigl[\hat P_\mathrm{ASM}(k_\perp;\Delta z)\,\mathcal{F}\{\psi(x,y,z)\}\bigr].
\end{equation}
Here $V_\mathrm{proj}(x,y)=\int_\mathrm{slice}V\,\mathrm{d}z$ is the projected potential energy and $\sigma$ is the corresponding relativistic interaction parameter. The transmission factor is identical to standard multislice. Replacing only the Fresnel kernel
costs nothing: the FFT pair is unchanged and the per-slice cost remains $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$. The
relationship to Fresnel multislice is made transparent by expanding the square-root operator,
\begin{equation*}
\sqrt{k_0^2 + \nabla_\perp^2} \;=\; k_0 + \frac{\nabla_\perp^2}{2k_0}
- \frac{\nabla_\perp^4}{8k_0^3} + \cdots,
\end{equation*}
where truncating at the second term recovers the Fresnel kernel
$\exp(-ik_\perp^2\Delta z/(2k_0))$, whereas ASM retains the operator to all orders. ASM is the
minimal correction to standard multislice that captures the correct free-space high-angle
propagation while preserving its split-step structure and FFT efficiency. It differs from the WPM
only in that, inside the potential, the wavelength is held at its vacuum value $\lambda_0=2\pi/k_0$
rather than tracking the local $\lambda(x,y)=2\pi/(k_0 n(x,y))$.

\subsection{Fresnel multislice}
\label{sec:fms}

For completeness we recall the standard split-step Fresnel multislice scheme derived in the
Introduction (equation~\eqref{eq:propagator_correspondence_intro}): the slice propagator combines a transmission factor
$\exp(i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj})$ with the paraxial Fresnel kernel
$\exp(-ik_\perp^2\Delta z/(2k_0))$, obtained by truncating the square-root operator at second order
in $k_\perp/k_0$. It is the cheapest of these schemes---one FFT pair per slice---and the
standard tool of quantitative TEM simulation. Its accuracy is bounded by the paraxial assumption
$k_\perp^2 \ll k_0^2$.

\section{Results}

We present two numerical experiments that isolate the high-angle behaviour most relevant to this
work. Both compare Fresnel multislice (FMS), angular spectrum multislice (ASMS), and the Wave
Propagation Method (WPM) against a converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon
equation. The first reproduces the Au~[100] beam-amplitude benchmark of Rother and
Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}; the second tests a convergent Au~[110] probe over a
wide range of semi-angles.

\subsection{Verification against Rother \& Scheerschmidt (2009)}
\label{sec:verification}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
    \caption{Exit-wave amplitude comparison for the Au~[100] verification case, showing the corresponding amplitudes of the fourier transform of the exit wave produced by each propagation model and by the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
\end{figure}

To verify our implementation, and assess which propagation methods retain accuracy at high angles, we recreated the simulation presented in figure~3 of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, which propagates a plane wave input through a thick gold [100] crystal at 300~keV. The ground truth is the converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon equation. In this example, periodic boundary conditions are implicit, due to each method using a Fourier transform solution, and as a result the crystal is infinitely wide. Since we wished to explore the accuracy of each method in thicker objects, we extended the simulation as presented by Rother and Scheerschmidt from 25 unit cells (~10nm thickness) to 100 unit cells (~40nm thickness) It is immediately obvious in \ref{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes}, which displays the amplitude of the fourier transform of the exit wave after propagation through 100 unit cells of the gold crystal, that the Fresnel Propagator method is less accurate than the three other methods. The angular spectrum multislice, WPM, and KG ODE solutions all closely track each other, with the the WPM solution appearing to track more accurately the amplitude of the pixels in the ground truth solution compared to the angular spectrum method. However, as noted by Rother and Scheerschmidt, the images can hardly be compared quantitatively by eye: as a result, we have repeated their experiment in figure-3 by plotting the normalised amplitude of the fourier transform of the exit wave after each slice for the two selected pixels - an on axis [0, 0] pixel in the centre of the image, and a high-angle [0, 28] pixel at ~135mrad from 0 to 25, and from 0 to 100 unit cells.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_pixel_amplitudes}
    \caption{Pixel-amplitude comparison for the Au~[100] verification case. The central and high-angle pixels are compared across Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, the Wave Propagation Method, and the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:au_pixel_amplitudes}
\end{figure}

For the central pixel [0, 0], all methods agree closely up to ~10nm thickness or 25 unit cells. We extented this simulation out to 100 unit cells or 40 nm thickness, and found that past 10nm the error between the fresnel propagator and all other methods begins to increase even for the on-axis [0,0] pixel. Past 15nm of thickness, there has been enough propagation through the crystal that higher angle diffracted beams can make their way back towards the central pixel and modify it's value. The fresnel propagator is not capable of tracking these beams, and thus it's accuracy suffers as a result. For the high-angle pixel [0, 28] at 135~mrad, the Fresnel multislice solution diverges from the ground truth after approximately 10 unit cells or 4nm, consistent again with the findings of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009} who attributed the discrepancy to the linearization of the square-root operator. Interestingly, the angular spectrum solution which requires only a small implementation change to the standard Fresnel Multislice method, tracks the ground truth much more closely than the Fresnel solution, and is comparable to the WPM solution which is significantly more expensive to implement and compute. It is only at a very large thickness of ~35 nm in the off axis pixel that it becomes clear the angular spectrum is starting to deviate from the ground truth. The WPM solution tracks the ground truth more closely than the angular spectrum method, but the difference is small and only becomes apparent at very large thicknesses. At least in this case, the extra computational effort of the wave propagation method does not appear to provide a significant accuracy improvement over the angular spectrum method.

\subsection{Convergent-probe Au CBED}
\label{sec:convergence_angle}


Next, we tested each method's accuracy as a function of probe convergence semi-angle for a convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) geometry, on a 3 times enlarged version of the Au~[100] unit cell in x and y compared to the unit cell used in the previous section up to a thickness of ~100 nm. In order to ensure accurate tracking of the wave for a large convergence angle, we increased the sampling to 256 x 256 over the enlarged unit cell. The probe is focused on the entrance surface of the specimen, so the initial wavefunction in amplitude is an airy function without any aberrations. The input wave is then propagated through the specimen using each method and compared to the KG ODE ground truth. The CBED diffraction pattern is calculated at the exit surface by taking the fourier transform of the exit wave and compared across methods.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps}
    \caption{CBED exit-wave amplitude comparison in log scale for the Au~[100] convergence-angle study. The maps show the final diffraction patterns produced by Fresnel multislice, angular spectrum multislice, the Wave Propagation Method, and the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps}
\end{figure}

A series of convergent probes including the following semi-angles - 25, 50, 100 mrad were propagated through 
using 128 slices per unit cell for each method. Figure~\ref{fig:cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps} again confirms that both the wave propagation method and the angular spectrum method are capable of tracking the high-angle scattering more accurately than the Fresnel multislice method. Visually the final CBED pattern of the Fresnel Propagator solution is noticeably worse than the WPM or Angular Spectrum methods. The WPM for all three convergence angles at the end of the propagation routine shows the smallest error in the CBED amplitude - staying on average at 3.5\% mean error comparaed to the ground truth ODE solution. The WPM solution tracks the ground truth more closely than the angular spectrum method, but again difference is small and only becomes apparent at very large thicknesses. Again, and also is this more extreme case, the extra computational effort of the wave propagation method does not appear to provide a significant accuracy improvement over the Angular Spectrum method.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{cbed_error_vs_thickness}
    \caption{Relative CBED error as a function of specimen thickness for the Au~[100] convergence-angle study. The error is reported against the Klein--Gordon ODE reference at the end of each unit cell for the tested probe semi-angles.}
    \label{fig:cbed_error_vs_thickness}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:cbed_error_vs_thickness} we visualise the root mean square (RMSE) error of the CBED intensity against the KG ODE reference as a function of object thickness, where we again see that after a thickness of approximately 10nm in all cases, there is an order of magnitude error difference in the RMSE of the Fresnel multislice method compared to the WPM and Angular Spectrum methods. The angular spectrum method does become less accurate as the thickness increases, but still remains closer to the accuracy of the WPM compared to the Fresnel Propagation Method. The WPM method tracks the ground truth more closely than the angular spectrum method and at least up to these thicknesses, maintains a consistent relative error. Repeatedly, there does not appear to be a compelling reason to use the WPM method over the angular spectrum method, given the small accuracy improvement and the significantly higher computational cost of the WPM method. Especially given the fact that the angular spectrum method is a simple modification to the standard Fresnel multislice method, and can be implemented with no additional computational cost, it appears to be the best choice for high-angle scattering simulations in TEM.

\section{Sampling Requirements}
\label{sec:sampling_requirements}

When replacing the Fresnel kernel with the angular spectrum kernel, the main
concern is a question of sampling. Are the typical existing FFT grid and slice thicknesses
still fine enough to represent the propagator? For Fresnel multislice this is
usually straightforward, because the kernel
$\exp(-i k_\perp^2 \Delta z / 2k_0)$ is smooth for all $k_\perp$. The angular
spectrum kernel $\exp(i\Delta z \sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2})$ is slightly more
delicate: its phase changes rapidly as $k_\perp$ approaches $k_0$, and for
$k_\perp > k_0$ the modes become evanescent. As a result, the usual
phase-sampling condition gives a slightly tighter slice-thickness bound for ASM
than for Fresnel. This factor comes from comparing how quickly the propagator
phase changes across transverse-frequency space. For one propagation step, the
Fresnel and ASM phases are

\[
\phi_{\mathrm{F}}(k_\perp) = -\frac{\Delta z}{2k_0}k_\perp^2,
\qquad
\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}(k_\perp) = \Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}.
\]

Their phase gradients are therefore

\[
\left|\frac{d\phi_{\mathrm{F}}}{dk_\perp}\right|
= \Delta z\frac{k_\perp}{k_0},
\qquad
\left|\frac{d\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}}{dk_\perp}\right|
= \Delta z\frac{k_\perp}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}.
\]

So the ASM phase varies faster by the ratio

\[
\frac{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}/dk_\perp\right|}{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{F}}/dk_\perp\right|}
= \frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}.
\]

Evaluating this at the largest represented transverse wavevector
$k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}$ gives the slightly tighter ASM slice-thickness bound,
by the factor

\[
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}^2}}.
\]

In conventional TEM this extra restriction is usually negligible. For the
200~keV case discussed here, the numbers are:

\[
\lambda \approx 0.025\,\text{\AA},
\qquad
k_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \approx \frac{2\pi}{0.025} \approx 250\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

For a 200~mrad probe, the largest transverse wavevector is

\[
k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}} = k_0 \sin\alpha
\approx 250\sin(0.2)
\approx 50\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

So the relevant ratio is

\[
\frac{k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}}{k_0} \approx \frac{50}{250} \approx 0.2.
\]

The extra ASM sampling penalty is therefore

\[
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}^2}}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}/k_0)^2}}
\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.2^2}}
\approx 1.02,
\]

which means the slice-thickness bound is tightened by only about 2\%.

The same conclusion holds for the transverse grid. If the real-space pixel size
is $\Delta x = 0.04\,\text{\AA}$, then the Nyquist spatial frequency is

\[
f_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{1}{2\Delta x} = \frac{1}{0.08} = 12.5\,\text{\AA}^{-1},
\]

so the corresponding transverse wavevector is

\[
k_{\perp,\mathrm{Nyq}} = 2\pi f_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{\pi}{\Delta x}
\approx \frac{\pi}{0.04}
\approx 78.5\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

This gives a Nyquist scattering angle of

\[
\theta_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{k_{\perp,\mathrm{Nyq}}}{k_0}\right)
\approx \arcsin\!\left(\frac{78.5}{250}\right)
\approx 0.32\,\text{rad}
\approx 320\,\text{mrad}.
\]

This is still well below the singular point at $k_\perp = k_0$. The
transmission factor $\exp(i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj})$ places the same sampling
demands in Fresnel and ASM, since it depends only on the projected potential.
In practice, this means that if a grid is already adequate for high-quality
Fresnel multislice in TEM, it is usually also adequate for angular spectrum
multislice. Extra ASM-specific sampling only becomes important in regimes much
closer to $k_\perp = k_0$, such as extreme near-field or soft-X-ray problems
rather than the electron-microscopy cases studied here.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a comparison of three split-step multislice
propagators of increasing physical complexity, Fresnel multislice, angular
spectrum multislice, and the Wave Propagation Method, benchmarked against a
direct forward-ODE solution of the full second-order Klein--Gordon equation in
two high-angle settings: the Au~[100] beam-amplitude verification of Rother and
Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, extended here from 25 to 100 unit
cells, and a convergent-beam diffraction sample in Au~[100] at probe semi-angles
up to 100~mrad and specimen thicknesses up to roughly 100~nm. In every case the
Klein--Gordon ODE served as a non-perturbative reference that makes no
split-step or paraxial approximation, allowing us to attribute the observed
discrepancies cleanly to the choice of propagator.

The central result is that replacing the Fresnel kernel with the exact angular
spectrum kernel recovers much of what is lost in the paraxial approximation.
In the Rother--Scheerschmidt verification, Fresnel multislice tracks the
Klein--Gordon reference at the central beam only up to about 10~nm of gold and
diverges from the 135~mrad off-axis beam after only $\sim$4~nm, in agreement
with the original paper from Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}. By contrast, the angular spectrum and Wave
Propagation methods both follow the ODE reference closely out to 40~nm, and
differ from one another only at the largest thicknesses. The convergent-probe
CBED study again confirms this result: across 25, 50 and 100~mrad
probe semi-angles, the relative CBED-amplitude error of Fresnel multislice
exceeds that of angular spectrum and WPM by roughly an order of magnitude
beyond $\sim$10~nm, while ASM and WPM more accurately reproduce the ODE
ground truth throughout. The WPM is consistently the most accurate of the
split-step schemes, holding an average error near 3.5\% in the CBED simulation
but its accuracy advantage over angular spectrum multislice is small in
absolute terms and comes at a substantially higher computational cost.

Taken together, these results indicate that the dominant missing physics in
conventional Fresnel multislice for TEM is non-paraxial free-space dispersion,
not the local, potential-dependent change of wavelength inside the specimen
that the WPM additionally captures. This is consistent with the sampling
analysis of Section~\ref{sec:sampling_requirements}: under typical TEM grids and
slice thicknesses, the angular spectrum kernel imposes only a few-percent
tighter sampling bound than the Fresnel kernel, and the Nyquist scattering
angle is far below the singular point $k_\perp = k_0$ where the angular
spectrum operator becomes brittle. The implication for electron microscopy in at least the
high energy regime we have discussed here is that any existing Fresnel multislice code can be upgraded to wide-angle
accuracy by changing a single line in the propagator---replacing
$\exp(-i k_\perp^2 \Delta z / 2k_0)$ with $\exp(i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2})$---without
changing the FFT grid, the slice thickness, the transmission step, or the
per-slice cost.

Looking forward, the Klein--Gordon ODE and WPM methods still provide an,
arbitrary-probe ground-truth reference that we expect to be useful beyond
the cases studied here, particularly for benchmarking new propagators and for exploring 
solutions where the local wavelength inside the medium is no longer approximated by
its vacuum value---very thick high-$Z$ specimens at low accelerating voltages,
soft-X-ray problems, and near-field optical settings. The differentiable,
quadratically-binned WPM implementation also keeps the method tractable on GPU
and compatible with automatic differentiation, which could make it a credible
forward model for gradient-based inverse problems such as multislice
ptychography in the future.

\section{Differentiable refractive-index binning for WPM multislice}
\label{sec:binning}

To make the computation of the wave propagation method tractable and limit the need for $O(N^{2D})$ computations, we adapt the WPM algorithm presented in \cite{DiffWPM2025} which
uses a refractive index binning strategy to limit the number of unique refractive index values in a slice while maintaining differentiability with respect to the refractive index map $n(x,y)$.

To implement this method, we first discretize the refractive index range $[n_{\mathrm{min}}, n_{\mathrm{max}}]$ of the current slice into $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ discrete reference values, denoted as
$\{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}}$.

In a typical multislice potential, many of the refractive index values will be very close to $1$ (vacuum region), while the refractive index values of an atom
will concentrate in a narrow range slightly above $1$. Since there are likely to be many more refractive index values near the vacuum region,
we employ a quadratic distribution for the reference indices rather than using linearly spaced bins in order to allocate more bins near $n=1$.
The reference indices are distributed according to a quadratic function determined by the parameter $\gamma$, with $\gamma=2.0$ for quadratic spacing:

\[
n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)} = n_{\mathrm{min}} + (n_{\mathrm{max}} - n_{\mathrm{min}}) \left( \frac{k}{N_{\mathrm{bins}}-1} \right)^\gamma
\]

which allows for denser binning near the vacuum refractive index when $\gamma > 1$.

Next, for a propagation step $\Delta z$, we compute a bank of propagated fields $u_k(x,y)$. Each field $u_k$ corresponds to the
diffraction of the input field $u_{\mathrm{in}}(x,y)$ assuming a homogeneous medium with refractive index $n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}$.
This is performed efficiently via batch FFT operations using the angular spectrum kernel:

\[
u_k(x,y) = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left( \mathcal{F}(u_{\mathrm{in}}) e^{i \Delta z \sqrt{(k_0 n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)})^2 - k_x^2 - k_y^2}} \right)
\]

To evaluate the field at a specific spatial coordinate $(x,y)$ where the local refractive index is $n(x,y)$,
we identify the two bounding reference bins such that $n_L \le n(x,y) < n_R$, where $n_L, n_R \in \{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}$.
We adopt a differentiable interpolation scheme by defining a local interpolation weight

\[
w_{\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{n(x,y) - n_L}{n_R - n_L}, \quad w_{\mathrm{raw}} \in [0, 1]
\]

To ensure smooth gradients and minimize discretization artifacts, we map this linear weight through a smoothstep activation function $S(w)$,
akin to the spatial partitioning technique demonstrated by \cite{BarreBrunel} via a cubic Hermite interpolation function:

\[
w = S(w_{\mathrm{raw}}) = 3w_{\mathrm{raw}}^2 - 2w_{\mathrm{raw}}^3
\]
While linear interpolation would satisfy the requirement for differentiability, it could result in discontinuities in the gradient at the bin boundaries in a gradient-based optimization scheme.

The final field at the next step $u(x,y, z+\Delta z)$ is computed as the weighted superposition of the fields
propagated at the bounding indices:

\[
u(x,y) = (1 - w) \cdot u_L(x,y) + w \cdot u_R(x,y)
\]

This formulation ensures that the propagation model is fully differentiable with respect to the refractive index map $n(x,y)$, enabling inverse reconstruction of potentials via automatic differentiation,
and that only a limited number of FFTs (equal to $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$) are required per propagation step, significantly reducing the computational cost compared to a naive WPM implementation.
The accuracy of this binned WPM multislice method depends on the number of bins $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ chosen and will change on a case-by-case basis depending on the refractive index distribution of the sample.
One can mitigate this potential source of error by performing a test simulation and doubling the number of bins until no difference is observed in the output wavefunction.

In general, the WPM will need a GPU for efficient computation, and we have found that $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ between 64 and 512 are sufficient for the simulations presented in this work.
This is a marked improvement over the naive case, as it enables the WPM to simulate many slices with larger grid sizes ($512 \times 512$ or $1024 \times 1024$ pixels)
in a reasonable time frame (seconds to minutes), provided the refractive index binning strategy is capable of correctly capturing the refractive index distribution of the sample.
