\begin{abstract}
Conventional Fresnel multislice (F-MS) is the standard method for modeling
electron--sample interactions in transmission electron microscopy (TEM). It is
computationally efficient and can simulate both perfect and defective crystals,
but its paraxial propagation kernel loses accuracy when large scattering angles
contribute to the exit wave. Here we compare FFT-based alternatives that retain
the practical split-step structure of multislice while improving wide-angle
propagation. We introduce wave-propagation multislice (WP-MS), an adaptation of
the wave propagation method from scalar optics, and compare it with F-MS and
angular-spectrum multislice (AS-MS). The three methods are first benchmarked
against a direct ordinary-differential-equation (ODE) solution of the
second-order Klein--Gordon Helmholtz equation using the on-axis and high-angle
beam amplitudes of Au~[100]. We then compare their predictions for
convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) from Si~[111] and Au~[100]. AS-MS
removes most of the wide-angle error of F-MS at essentially the same per-slice
computational cost. WP-MS is closest to the ODE reference, but its additional
accuracy over AS-MS is modest under the high-energy conditions studied here.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The traditional image simulation workflow in electron microscopy assumes
a structural model, calculates an image from that model,
and qualitatively compares the agreement with experiment via
trial and error. In modern reconstruction workflows, including
ptychography and related phase-retrieval methods, the recorded experimental
data are now used to
recover a projected potential, phase shift, or three-dimensional structure, where the
electron--matter interaction model is not merely a tool for visual
comparison after the experiment, but part of the reconstruction algorithm
itself. The accuracy of the forward model is therefore of critical importance to
the accuracy of the recovered structure.

At the most fundamental level, electron propagation through matter is a
time-dependent many-body quantum problem. The incident electron interacts with
the specimen electrons and nuclei, so a complete description would include
elastic and inelastic scattering channels, multiple scattering, possible energy
exchange with the specimen, and in principle both forward- and
backward-propagating components. Such a direct treatment is not practical for
realistic TEM specimens, and image simulations instead rely on reduced
stationary wave equations. Multislice methods provide such a practical numerical 
tool to simulate the electron-specimen interaction. 

The standard implementation is paraxial Fresnel multislice (F-MS)
~\cite{cowley1957scattering,ishizuka1977practical}. F-MS is simple, fast, and
well suited to conventional high-energy TEM and STEM simulations of thin,
low-$Z$ specimens. In many experimental comparisons, uncertainties such as
thermal diffuse scattering, detector response, specimen drift, or imperfect
aberration calibration dominate, so F-MS is often adequate.
However, for low incident energies, thick specimens, high-$Z$ materials, or
simulations that retain high-angle diffraction information, F-MS can introduce
significant errors because its propagation kernel is paraxial and because
standard forward multislice neglects backscattering
~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009,hillier_role_2017,WackerSchroeder2015,ming2013pcms}.

The smallest correction to F-MS is to replace the parabolic Fresnel propagator
between slices by the exact one-way spherical-wave propagator. As far as we are aware, 
Ming et al.~\cite{ming2013pcms} were the first to explicitly propose the full spherical propagator
correction in electron electron microscopy. We refer to
this method as angular-spectrum multislice (AS-MS), following the terminology
used in scalar light optics~\cite{goodman}. AS-MS keeps the same split-step structure
and the same FFT-based cost as F-MS, but restores the exact free-space
dispersion relation. It is therefore a minimal wide-angle correction, although
it still treats the specimen as a sequence of projected phase gratings
separated by vacuum propagation.

More complete wide-angle methods seek to improve the propagation inside the
specimen, or to use the full second-order wave equation as a reference. The
Chen--Van Dyck method, also known as fully corrected multislice (FC-MS),
expands the square-root propagation operator in a Taylor series and retains
higher-order terms that couple the specimen potential to transverse
derivatives~\cite{ChenVanDyck1997,ming2013pcms}. This improves the treatment of
large scattering angles, low-energy electrons, thick specimens, and high-$Z$
materials, and in its more complete form gives access to backward-propagating
components. The cost is that the square-root operator is no longer a simple
Fourier-space phase factor, and can be expensive to compute depending of the number 
of terms chosen to solve in the series expansion.

A closely related method to that presented by Chen--Van Dyck was studied earlier in scalar light optics, and are known as
Pad\'e beam-propagation methods, which approximate the same square-root operator by rational
functions rather than Taylor polynomials~\cite{Hadley1992}. These
approximants can represent the square-root dispersion relation over a wider
angular range than a truncated Taylor series of the same order, but they still
introduce substantial overhead relative to F-MS or AS-MS. 
Finally, direct integration of
the full second-order wave equation as an ordinary differential equation (ODE)
provides a more accurate numerical reference, and has been used to assess
paraxial and corrected multislice methods
~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009,WackerSchroeder2015}. These studies indicate
that a dominant high-angle error of F-MS arises from the parabolic propagation
step. Hillier et al.~\cite{hillier_role_2017} also showed that retaining the
second longitudinal derivative is important for the positions of Laue-zone
rings in low-energy Si CBED patterns. However, ODE and higher-order corrected
methods are too expensive to use directly as routine forward models in
large-scale inverse reconstructions.

In order to overcome the issue of needing an accurate and efficient solution to the Time Independent
wave equation, the light-optics community has developed alternative methods that retain the efficiency 
of the FFT while avoiding the need to choose a Taylor or Pad\'e expansion order.
One such method is the wave propagation method (WPM), introduced by Brenner and
Singer~\cite{Brenner1993}. WPM treats the medium as locally homogeneous over
small transverse regions, applies homogeneous angular-spectrum propagators for
representative refractive indices, and recombines the resulting fields
according to the local refractive-index distribution. This retains a
potential-dependent wavelength within the propagation step without explicitly
forming the full dense square-root operator. Efficient and differentiable
variants have been developed in scalar optics literature ~\cite{Schmidt2016,BarreBrunel2025}.
The method is therefore a natural starting point for a practical wide-angle
multislice algorithm in electron microscopy, but to our knowledge it has not
yet been systematically assessed in this context.

The aim of this work is to adapt the WPM from the light optics community (which wec call Wave Propagation Multislice, WP-MS) and assess it's benefits in the context of electron microscopy.
We therefore compare F-MS, AS-MS, and the wave-propagation multislice method (WP-MS) against a
forward ODE reference solution of the full second-order Klein--Gordon Helmholtz
equation, and then test the FFT-based methods in representative Si~[111] and
Au~[100] CBED simulations. The comparison identifies when F-MS remains
sufficient, when the simple AS-MS correction captures the dominant error, and
if the local-wavelength correction in WP-MS becomes relevant.

\section{Wave-Equation Models and Propagation Algorithms}
\label{sec:propagation_models}

\subsection{Wave equation and refractive index}
\label{sec:wave_equation_refractive_index}

Throughout this work we use spatial frequencies in cycles per unit length,
rather than angular wavenumbers in radians per unit length. The vacuum spatial
frequency is therefore
\begin{equation}
k_0=\frac{1}{\lambda},
\label{eq:spatial_frequency_convention}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the relativistic electron wavelength. We use the
Fourier transform convention
\begin{equation}
\mathcal F\{f(x,y)\}(k_x,k_y)
=
\int f(x,y)
\exp\left[-2\pi i(k_xx+k_yy)\right]\,dx\,dy,
\label{eq:fourier_convention}
\end{equation}
and write $k_\perp^2=k_x^2+k_y^2$.

For high-energy elastic scattering in an electrostatic potential, we start from
the scalar relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger equation
~\cite{fujiwara1961relativistic,kirkland2010advanced}
\begin{equation}
\left[
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\gamma m_0}\nabla^2
+
E_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]\psi(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{p_0^2}{2\gamma m_0}\psi(\mathbf r),
\label{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro}
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ is the spatial wavefunction, $m_0$ is the electron rest mass,
$\gamma$ is the Lorentz factor, and $p_0=h/\lambda=h k_0$ is the vacuum
momentum. We write the electrostatic potential in volts as $V(\mathbf r)$ and
the corresponding electron potential energy as
$E_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)=-eV(\mathbf r)$, with $e>0$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro} can be written in
Helmholtz form as~\cite{Reimer1989}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:helmholtz_intro}
\nabla^2\psi+(2\pi k_0)^2 n^2(\mathbf r)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
The corresponding refractive index is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:index_schrodinger_intro}
n_\mathrm{S}^2(\mathbf r)
=
1-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{h^2k_0^2}E_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
=
1+\frac{\sigma}{\pi k_0}V(\mathbf r),
\end{equation}
where the conventional TEM interaction constant is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:interaction_constant}
\sigma
=
\frac{2\pi\gamma m_0e}{h^2k_0}
=
\frac{2\pi\gamma m_0e\lambda}{h^2}.
\end{equation}

The identification of the electrostatic potential with an electron-optical
refractive index is due to~\cite{Glaser1933, Ehrenberg-Siday1949, Reimer1989}.
The refractive index notation is useful because the choice of scalar wave equation can be
expressed through the choice of $n(\mathbf r)$, separating approximations to the
wave equation from approximations to the potential. For comparison, the
time-independent Klein--Gordon equation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:index_kg_intro}
n_\mathrm{KG}^2(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{
\left[E_{\mathrm{tot}}-E_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)\right]^2-m_0^2c^4
}{
E_{\mathrm{tot}}^2-m_0^2c^4
}.
\end{equation}
Here $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the incident-electron energy in vacuum including the
rest energy, so for an electron accelerated through a voltage $U$,
$E_{\mathrm{tot}}=m_0c^2+eU=\gamma m_0c^2$.
 
The relativistically
corrected Schr\"odinger refractive index is a linearised potential
approximation to the Klein--Gordon refractive index, while retaining the relativistic
vacuum momentum through $k_0$. Therefore changing
from $n_\mathrm{S}$ to $n_\mathrm{KG}$ changes the potential model, whereas the
choice between Fresnel, angular-spectrum, and WP-MS propagation changes how a
given input field through a refractive-index distribution is propagated.

\subsection{Fresnel and angular-spectrum multislice}
\label{sec:fresnel_angular_spectrum}

To connect equation~\eqref{eq:helmholtz_intro} to conventional multislice, we
make the standard high-energy, or slowly varying envelope approximation used in
electron microscopy~\cite{kirkland2010advanced}. Writing the wavefunction as a
rapidly oscillating carrier multiplied by a slowly varying envelope,
\begin{equation}
\psi(x,y,z)=\exp(2\pi i k_0z)\tilde\psi(x,y,z),
\label{eq:carrier_envelope}
\end{equation}
gives the parabolic wave equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:paraxial_helmholtz_intro}
\frac{\partial\tilde\psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{4\pi k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\tilde\psi
+
i\pi k_0\left[n^2(x,y,z)-1\right]\tilde\psi .
\end{equation}
For the corrected Schr\"odinger model, the interaction term reduces to
$i\sigma V\tilde\psi$. For slice $j$, define the projected electrostatic
potential and transmission function by
\begin{equation}
V_j(x,y)
=
\int_{z_j}^{z_j+\Delta z} V(x,y,z)\,dz,
\qquad
t_j(x,y)
=
\exp\left[i\sigma V_j(x,y)\right].
\label{eq:multislice_transmission}
\end{equation}

Fresnel multislice (F-MS) applies this transmission function and then
propagates the wave through vacuum with the paraxial Fresnel kernel. Dropping
the tilde for notational simplicity, one slice can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fresnel_step}
\psi_{j+1}(x,y)
\approx
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
\exp\left(-i\pi\frac{k_\perp^2}{k_0}\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{t_j(x,y)\psi_j(x,y)\}
\right].
\end{equation}
Computing the above equation is fast because each slice requires only one FFT pair. Its
approximation is that the free-space longitudinal wavevector
has been expanded as
\[
k_z
=
\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}
\simeq
k_0-\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}.
\]
After removal of the carrier phase $\exp(2\pi i k_0z)$, this expansion gives the
Fresnel kernel in equation~\eqref{eq:fresnel_step}. Conventional multislice is
therefore both a split-step approximation to the specimen and a small-angle
approximation to the longitudinal dispersion relation.

The angular spectrum method keeps the same split-step structure but replaces the
Fresnel kernel by the exact free-space dispersion relation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_step}
\psi_{j+1}(x,y)
\approx
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
\exp\left(2\pi i\left[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right]\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{t_j(x,y)\psi_j(x,y)\}
\right].
\end{equation}
Apart from the change in the Fourier-space phase factor, the algorithm is the
same as standard multislice and has the same $\mathcal O(P\log P)$ per-slice
cost, where $P$ is the number of transverse pixels. It is therefore the smallest
change one can make to F-MS in order to restore the exact
free-space dispersion relation. The approximation that remains is the split-step
treatment of the specimen: the potential is still applied as a thin transmission
function, while propagation between slices is still performed in vacuum.

\subsection{Classical Wave Propagation Method in scalar optics}
\label{sec:wpm_optics}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{method_explainer_generations/wpm}
    \caption{Schematic of the Wave Propagation Method (WPM) as presented by \cite{Schmidt2016}. 
    The input wave is
    propagated using a bank of homogeneous angular-spectrum kernels, each
    evaluated at a different representative refractive index $n_m$. At each
    output pixel, the propagated field corresponding to the local refractive
    index is selected via mask and recombined. Our improvement to this algorithm (not described in this figure
    for simplicity) includes a 
    soft binning step which makes this method suitable for a continuous refractive index distribution - see Figure \ref{fig:wpm_binning_diagnostics} below.}
    \label{fig:wpm_explainer}
\end{figure}

The Wave Propagation Method (WPM) addresses the remaining split-step
approximation in AS-MS by allowing the propagation kernel itself to depend on
the refractive index of the medium. Returning to the full second-order
Helmholtz equation \eqref{eq:helmholtz_intro}, it can be rearranged into an operator equation with a forward and backward branch \cite{Schmidt2016}. Ignoring the backward branch, the forward branch may be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}
\partial_z\psi
=
2\pi i\left[
\sqrt{k_0^2n^2(x,y,z)+\frac{1}{4\pi^2}\nabla_\perp^2}-k_0
\right]\psi .
\end{equation}
The central concern is that the square-root operator inside the wave equation
mixes the potential and the transverse derivative, and cannot be evaluated as a
single Fourier-space propagator. Pad\'e BPM and FC-MS methods solve for
this operator using rational or series approximations to the square-root term,
which are expensive and cannot usually directly use the FFT.

Looking again at the AS-MS propagation kernel of equation \eqref{eq:asm_step},
\[
\left[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right]
\]
what is primarily missing is a refractive-index term. We can reintroduce this term locally
by replacing $k_0$ with $k_0n(x,y)$, giving

\[
\left[\sqrt{k_0^2n^2(x,y)-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right].
\]

For a single refractive-index value, this kernel can be evaluated
straightforwardly with the angular spectrum method. The traditional WPM as introduced
by Brenner simply extends the
angular spectrum method by evaluating a bank of homogeneous angular-spectrum
propagators for each refractive index in the slice, and then retaining the
propagated field corresponding to the local refractive index at each output
pixel. 

This operation is obviously simplistic, because output pixels can receive a
propagated field that travelled from some pixels with the incorrect refractive
index. However, the approximation lives in the fact that those different
refractive indices are further away from the local refractive-index value of
that pixel, and therefore contribute less to the final result. In this manner,
the propagation step accounts for the local wavelength inside the specimen
without explicitly evaluating the full square-root operator. Schmidt
et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016} provided a more rigorous 
derivation motivating the WPM, and showed that the leading error from neglecting coupling
between neighbouring refractive-index regions affects amplitude rather than
phase; consequently, the WPM phase propagation is correct up to second order.

Figure~\ref{fig:wpm_explainer} shows the same local-homogeneous idea in the
form used by our electron-microscopy adaptation below. In the classical WPM as
implemented by Schmidt, a mask is created to separate out each unique refractive index in a
slice, the angular spectrum kernel is calculated for each refractive index, and
then the mask is used to choose the correct angular spectrum solution from the
bank of all computed propagations. Schmidt et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016} made the method practical
for many optical simulations by grouping pixels with the same refractive
index, as only one homogeneous angular-spectrum propagation is then required for
each distinct index value. If a slice contains $M$ such values, the cost is
reduced to approximately
\[
\mathcal O(MP\log P),
\]
instead of a direct all-to-all evaluation.

This saving is large in light-optical simulations where a slice may contain only
a small number of unique refractive indices. It is less immediate in electron
microscopy, where the electrostatic potential varies continuously and almost
every pixel may have a different refractive index. Exact grouping therefore
gives little benefit unless the refractive-index field is deliberately
discretised. A second issue is that hard grouping introduces sharp masks, which
are inconvenient for gradient-based optimisation because the assignment of pixels
to refractive-index groups is not smoothly differentiable.

\subsection{Wave propagation multislice}
\label{sec:wp_ms}

We refer to our electron-microscopy adaptation of WPM as wave propagation
multislice (WP-MS), to distinguish it from the classical WPM used in scalar
light optics. The main practical issue is that an electron-microscopy slice has
a nearly continuous refractive-index distribution, so grouping exactly by
refractive-index value would require almost one homogeneous propagation
per pixel. We therefore use a differentiable binned version of WPM, partly
following the soft-partition idea of~\cite{BarreBrunel2025}, but generalised
from two refractive-index values to many reference values.

For each slice, we first discretise the refractive-index range
$[n_{\mathrm{min}}, n_{\mathrm{max}}]$ into
$N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ discrete reference values, denoted as
$\{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}}$.

Most pixels in a typical slice are close to vacuum, with $n\simeq 1$, whereas
the larger refractive-index values associated with atoms occupy a smaller
fraction of the slice. We therefore use non-uniform reference values rather
than linearly spaced bins, allocating more bins near $n=1$. The reference
indices are distributed according to

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bin_spacing}
n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}
=
n_{\mathrm{min}}
+
(n_{\mathrm{max}} - n_{\mathrm{min}})
\left( \frac{k-1}{N_{\mathrm{bins}}-1} \right)^\gamma,
\quad
k=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{bins}},
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=2$ in the simulations below. Values $\gamma>1$ give denser
binning near the vacuum refractive index. Figure~\ref{fig:wpm_binning_diagnostics}
shows this binning step for a representative slice.

Next, for a propagation step $\Delta z$, we compute a bank of propagated fields
$u_k(x,y)$. Each field $u_k$ corresponds to the diffraction of the input field
$u_{\mathrm{in}}(x,y)$ assuming a homogeneous medium with refractive index
$n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}$. This is performed efficiently using batch FFT
operations with the angular spectrum kernel:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_binned_fields}
u_k(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
\left[
\mathcal{F}\{u_{\mathrm{in}}\}
\exp\left(
2\pi i \Delta z
\left[\sqrt{(k_0 n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)})^2-k_x^2-k_y^2}-k_0\right]
\right)
\right].
\end{equation}

To evaluate the field at a specific spatial coordinate $(x,y)$ where the local
refractive index is $n(x,y)$, we identify the two bounding reference bins such
that $n_L \le n(x,y) < n_R$, where
$n_L,n_R \in \{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}$. We adopt a differentiable interpolation
scheme by defining a local interpolation weight

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_raw_weight}
w_{\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{n(x,y) - n_L}{n_R - n_L}, \quad w_{\mathrm{raw}} \in [0, 1].
\end{equation}

To obtain smooth gradients at the bin boundaries, we map this linear weight
through the cubic smoothstep function

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_smoothstep}
w = S(w_{\mathrm{raw}}) = 3w_{\mathrm{raw}}^2 - 2w_{\mathrm{raw}}^3.
\end{equation}

Linear interpolation is already differentiable inside each bin, but its
gradient is discontinuous at the bin boundaries.

The final field at the next step $u(x,y,z+\Delta z)$ is computed as the
weighted superposition of the fields propagated at the bounding indices:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_two_bin_interpolation}
u(x,y) = (1 - w) \cdot u_L(x,y) + w \cdot u_R(x,y).
\end{equation}

The local refractive index first determines the two bounding reference
indices $n_L$ and $n_R$, and the weight $w$ specifies the fractional position
of $n(x,y)$ between those two values after the smoothstep mapping. The fields
$u_L$ and $u_R$ are the full propagated fields obtained by assuming homogeneous
media with refractive indices $n_L$ and $n_R$, respectively, and the output
pixel is formed from their local two-bin mixture. 

Equivalently, the
WP-MS update can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bank}
\begin{aligned}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
&\approx
\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}} w_m(x,y)
\mathcal F^{-1}
\Bigl[
\exp\Bigl(
2\pi i
\bigl[
\sqrt{k_0^2(n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(m)})^2-k_\perp^2}
-k_0
\bigr]\Delta z
\Bigr)
\\
&\hspace{0.44\textwidth}
\times \mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This gives a differentiable approximation to the one-way medium propagator in
equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}. When the slice is homogeneous,
only one refractive-index bin is needed and WP-MS reduces to the angular
spectrum method. For an inhomogeneous specimen, the method is more expensive
than AS-MS because many FFT-based propagations are required per slice, but it
accounts for the local wavelength inside the specimen rather than propagating
every slice with the vacuum wavelength.

Because the binning and interpolation are smooth, the update is differentiable
with respect to the refractive-index map $n(x,y)$ and can be used in automatic
differentiation workflows. The cost is set by the number of bins because each
propagation step requires $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ FFT-based propagations rather than
one propagation as in AS-MS. This is still much cheaper than evaluating a
separate propagation for every distinct pixel value. The accuracy therefore
depends on whether the chosen bins resolve the refractive-index distribution of
the slice. In practice, we check this by increasing $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ until
the slice error reaches the required tolerance.

separate the binning construction into the interval assignment and the
interpolation coordinate. In
panel (a), the lower-bin map identifies the reference interval used at each
pixel, whereas panel (b) gives the position of the true refractive index inside
that interval through the interpolation weight. The diagnostic
refractive-index fit,
$n_{\mathrm{fit}}=(1-w)n_L+w n_R$, uses the same two-bin interpolation as the
field update. Panels (c), (e), and (f) show that the chosen non-uniform binning
resolves the refractive-index distribution for this slice, while panel (d)
shows that the residual interpolation error is spatially concentrated around
steep atomic peaks. This supports replacing a separate propagation for every
distinct pixel value by a finite bank of propagated fields.

In practice, WP-MS is most useful with GPU acceleration. For the simulations in
this work, $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ between 16 and 128 was sufficient for
$512 \times 512$ and $1024 \times 1024$ grids, provided that the binning
resolved the refractive-index distribution of the sample.

\subsection{Forward ODE reference}
\label{sec:forward_ode_reference}

We use an ordinary differential equation (ODE) reference obtained by applying a
method-of-lines discretisation to the second-order Klein--Gordon Helmholtz
equation. The transverse coordinates are represented on the same laterally
periodic FFT grid used by the multislice methods, while $z$ is left as the
continuous evolution variable. The transverse pixel size and field of view
therefore set the retained transverse spatial frequencies, or equivalently the
largest scattering angles represented on the grid. The method does not attempt
to resolve the rapidly oscillating electron wavelength in all three spatial
directions on a Cartesian mesh; instead, the adaptive ODE integration resolves
the rapid longitudinal oscillation along $z$.

Substituting $n_\mathrm{KG}$ into the Helmholtz equation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_second_order}
\partial_z^2\psi
=
-\left[\nabla_\perp^2+(2\pi k_0)^2n_\mathrm{KG}^2(x,y,z)\right]\psi .
\end{equation}
Introducing $\phi=\partial_z\psi$ gives the equivalent first-order system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_state}
\frac{d}{dz}
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi\\
\phi
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\phi\\
-\left[\nabla_\perp^2+(2\pi k_0)^2n_\mathrm{KG}^2(x,y,z)\right]\psi
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We integrate this system with an adaptive high-order Runge--Kutta method,
using the Dopri8 scheme in the implementation, with a maximum internal step
size set from the largest retained discrete frequency. The transverse Laplacian
is evaluated spectrally using FFTs, while the $n_\mathrm{KG}^2$ term is applied
by pointwise multiplication in real space. The discrete potential
supplied to the solver is interpreted as the slice-averaged electrostatic
potential, so $n_\mathrm{KG}^2$ is constant in $z$ within each slice and
discontinuous only at slice boundaries. The adaptive step-size controller is
clipped at those boundaries, so no accepted ODE step crosses a jump in the
right-hand side.
Thus, for a fixed transverse grid and slice stack, the ODE reference solves the
corresponding periodic FFT-grid Klein--Gordon problem to the specified ODE
tolerance. 

To initialise the calculation on the forward-propagating vacuum branch, the
entrance wave $\psi(z=0)$ is supplemented by the longitudinal derivative
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_phi0}
\phi(z=0)
=
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
2\pi i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,
\mathcal F\{\psi(z=0)\}
\right],
\end{equation}
where the square-root branch is chosen to give the forward-propagating vacuum
solution, and the decaying branch for any evanescent Fourier components on the
grid. In our implementation, the same condition is used when no initial
$\partial_z\psi$ is supplied; when the ODE solve is restarted slice by slice or
cell by cell, the returned exit derivative must be carried forward as the next
initial derivative. Otherwise, only $\psi$ is propagated and the second-order
state has been changed.

The entrance condition is one-way, but the subsequent evolution is not a
one-way propagation approximation. After $z=0$, the state contains both
$\psi$ and $\partial_z\psi$, and the second-order system can generate and carry
the backward-propagating component allowed by the discretised initial-value
problem.
In the Au~[100] benchmark, this calculation is applied one unit cell at a time:
the ODE solve is restarted for the next repeated cell using both the exit
wavefunction and the exit derivative from the previous cell. Thus the complete
second-order state is retained across unit cells, rather than being projected
back onto the forward vacuum branch at each cell boundary. However, the
calculation is still forward initialised at the entrance of the crystal and does
not solve a global two-sided scattering problem in which reflections from later
depths propagate back into earlier depths. Under the high-energy conditions
considered here, we therefore use the ODE result as a non-paraxial forward
reference, rather than as a quantitative backscattering calculation. Given the high-energy and weak-potential conditions, the backward-propagating
component is expected to be small, and the ODE reference is an accurate benchmark for the forward-propagating multislice methods.

With this reference defined, the three multislice methods and the ODE benchmark
can be seen asdifferent approximations within the same scalar Helmholtz wave equation framework, once
the refractive-index model is fixed. In the ODE benchmark below, all methods
use the same slice-wise $n_\mathrm{KG}$ map.
F-MS uses a split-step specimen approximation together with the paraxial
free-space propagator, AS-MS keeps the same split-step structure but restores
the exact free-space spherical propagator, and WP-MS remains one-way but
approximates propagation inside the medium by local homogeneous propagation.
By contrast, the ODE reference keeps the second-order Klein--Gordon Helmholtz
equation after transverse discretisation and evolves the transverse Laplacian
and specimen potential simultaneously, which is why it is used as the reference
calculation in the first numerical experiment.
See Table~\ref{tab:method_timeline} for a summary timeline of the methods
presented in this work.

\section{Benchmarks and Results}
\label{sec:results}

We use three numerical experiments to validate our methods against an ODE reference and to benchmark them against each other. 
The first method reproduces the Au~[100] beam-amplitude
benchmark of Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, so that
F-MS, AS-MS, and WP-MS can be compared with an ODE solution of the full
second-order Klein--Gordon equation. The ODE reference is not used in the
remaining tests because of its high computational cost. Instead, the second
experiment compares the FFT-based methods in a lower-$Z$ Si~[111] CBED
calculation, and the third uses a thick Au~[100] convergent-probe calculation
with WP-MS as the practical high-angle reference. This section reports the
observed numerical differences; their common interpretation and practical
consequences are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Au~[100] ODE Benchmark}
\label{sec:verification}

To verify the implementation, we recreated the simulation presented in
Figure~3 of Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}. The
benchmark propagates a plane wave through an Au~[100] crystal at 300~keV and
compares against a converged ODE solution of the full second-order
Klein--Gordon equation. Periodic lateral boundary conditions are implicit
because each method uses Fourier propagation, so the crystal is repeated in the
transverse directions. To test a thicker object, we extended the original
Rother--Scheerschmidt calculation from 25 unit cells ($\sim$10~nm) to 100 unit
cells ($\sim$40~nm).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
    \caption{Au~[100] ODE verification case after 100 unit cells. The left
    columns show the real-space exit-wave intensity and phase, and the rightmost
    column shows the HRTEM image intensity after applying a representative
    aberration-corrected 300~keV transfer function with residual
    $C_s=10~\mu\mathrm{m}$, defocus of $+6.0$~nm, and a 12~mrad objective
    aperture. Color scales are shared across methods within each column, and
    image-plane NRMSE values are relative to the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes} shows the exit-wave intensity, phase,
and transfer-function image intensity after propagation through 100 unit cells
of Au~[100]. The F-MS exit-wave amplitude and phase are qualitatively different
from AS-MS, WP-MS, and the Klein--Gordon ODE reference, whereas the latter
three remain visually similar. After the microscope transfer function is
applied, however, the image-plane differences are much less obvious because the
objective aperture and transfer function suppress part of the high-angle
information. The unfiltered exit wave is therefore the more sensitive
comparison for this benchmark.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_pixel_amplitudes}
    \caption{Fourier pixel-amplitude comparison for the Au~[100] verification

    \label{fig:au_pixel_amplitudes}

Figure~\ref{fig:au_pixel_amplitudes} gives the corresponding Fourier-amplitude
comparison after each slice. For the central [0, 0] component, all methods
agree closely up to approximately 10~nm, or 25 unit cells. Beyond this
thickness, the F-MS result begins to deviate from the other methods even on the
on-axis pixel. By about 15~nm, high-angle diffracted beams have propagated far
enough through the crystal to interfere back into the central beam, so an error
in their accumulated phase also modifies the on-axis amplitude.

For the high-angle [0, 28] component at approximately 135~mrad, the F-MS
solution diverges from the ODE reference after approximately 10 unit cells, or
4~nm, consistent with the findings of
Rother and Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}. AS-MS tracks the ODE
reference much more closely than F-MS and remains comparable to WP-MS. WP-MS is
slightly closer to the reference, but the difference between AS-MS and WP-MS is
only clear at the largest thicknesses considered here.

\subsection{Si~[111] CBED Peak Positions}
\label{sec:silicon_cbed}

After verifying AS-MS and WP-MS against the ODE solution, we focus on the three
FFT-based multislice methods: F-MS, AS-MS, and WP-MS. The first CBED test uses
Si~[111] at 100~keV with an 8~mrad probe and a specimen thickness near
1000~\AA, following the spirit of the low-energy CBED comparison of
Hillier et al.~\cite{hillier_role_2017} but at a higher accelerating voltage.
The maximum scattering angle was set to 300~mrad.

Figure~\ref{fig:si111_cbed_combined} shows the WP-MS CBED pattern, the radial
profiles with detected peaks, and the peak displacements relative to WP-MS. The
F-MS peaks shift progressively with scattering angle. By contrast, AS-MS tracks
WP-MS closely, with peak displacements near zero across the full 300~mrad

In Figure~\ref{fig:si111_cbed_combined}(c), the detected peak shifts are
compared with a rescaled dispersion-relation phase error, namely the difference
between the parabolic Fresnel dispersion and the exact spherical dispersion,
\[
|\Delta\phi|
= \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
\left|\sqrt{1-\lambda^2 q^2} - 1 + \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 q^2\right|,
\]
where $q=\sin\theta/\lambda$ is the transverse spatial frequency. This is the
longitudinal phase error introduced when the exact spherical propagator is
replaced by its Fresnel approximation. After fitting only the overall scale, the
curve follows the F-MS $-$ WP-MS peak shifts closely, indicating that the
observed displacement is mainly caused by accumulated free-space propagation
error between slices. In this Si case, the additional WP-MS correction from the
potential-dependent wavelength inside the specimen is not clearly resolved in
the peak positions.

\subsection{Au~[100] CBED Amplitude Errors}
\label{sec:au_cbed}

To test whether a higher atomic number increases the differences between the
FFT-based methods, we simulated convergent-beam electron diffraction from an
Au~[100] supercell at 200~keV using a 5~mrad aberration-free probe. The probe
was propagated to a specimen thickness of approximately 100~nm. To sample the
high-angle components of the convergent beam accurately, the enlarged
$9 \times 9$ gold cell was discretized on a $2048 \times 2048$ grid. We then
propagated the wave using F-MS, AS-MS, and WP-MS, and compared the
exit-surface CBED patterns obtained from the Fourier transform of each exit
wave.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The three benchmarks point to a common source of error in conventional
multislice under the high-angle, thick-specimen conditions tested here. In the
Au~[100] ODE benchmark, the F-MS discrepancy appears first in the 135~mrad beam
and only later in the central beam, after diffracted components have propagated
far enough through the crystal to interfere back into it. In the Si~[111] CBED
calculation, the F-MS peak shifts follow the parabolic-versus-exact dispersion
error, while AS-MS remains close to WP-MS over the measured angular range. In
the thick Au~[100] CBED calculation, the F-MS amplitude error becomes roughly
an order of magnitude larger than the AS-MS error beyond about 10~nm. Taken
together, these observations indicate that the dominant F-MS error in these
tests is the accumulated longitudinal phase error from the parabolic Fresnel
propagator.

\subsection{AS-MS as the minimal wide-angle correction}

AS-MS removes most of this error by changing only the free-space propagation
kernel. The transmission step, split-step ordering, FFT structure, and number
of FFTs per slice are the same as in F-MS. In the spatial-frequency
convention used here, the replacement is
\[
\exp(-i\pi k_\perp^2 \Delta z/k_0)
\quad\rightarrow\quad
\exp\left(2\pi i\Delta z[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0]\right).
\]
The correction is small when all important Fourier components satisfy
$k_\perp\ll k_0$, but it becomes significant when the simulation retains
components with large $k_\perp/k_0=\sin\theta$. Once those components are
present, the error is not confined to high-angle beams because dynamical
scattering can later mix them back into lower-angle amplitudes.

This is the main practical result of the comparison. If a simulation grid
already represents the high-angle scattering of interest, then replacing the
Fresnel kernel by the angular-spectrum kernel removes the dominant wide-angle
propagation error in these benchmarks without changing the basic multislice
workflow. Under those conditions, there is little practical reason to retain
the Fresnel propagator except for direct comparison with legacy calculations or
for regimes where the paraxial error is already negligible relative to other
uncertainties.

\subsection{Sampling and cost}

The practical advantage of AS-MS would be less useful if the exact
angular-spectrum phase required a substantially finer grid than F-MS. The
additional sampling concern comes from the angular-spectrum propagation phase,
which is steeper than the Fresnel phase at large transverse wavevector. For one
propagation step,

\[
\phi_{\mathrm{F}}(k_\perp) = -\pi\frac{\Delta z}{k_0}k_\perp^2,
\qquad
\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}(k_\perp) =
2\pi\Delta z\left[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right].
\]

Comparing the phase gradients gives the relative axial sampling penalty

\[
\frac{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}/dk_\perp\right|}
{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{F}}/dk_\perp\right|}
=
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}.
\]

This factor is only large when $k_\perp$ approaches $k_0$, where the
propagating spectrum approaches the evanescent boundary. It can be written
directly in terms of scattering angle because $k_\perp/k_0=\sin\theta$:

\[
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}}
= \frac{1}{\cos\theta}.
\]

Under the TEM conditions used here this remains close to unity. For a
200~mrad probe, the largest correction to the Fresnel slice-thickness
condition is

\[
\frac{1}{\cos(200~\mathrm{mrad})}
\approx 1.02,
\]

so the axial sampling bound is tightened by only about 2\%. The transverse
grid sets a separate limit because it determines the largest
scattering angle that can be represented without aliasing. A real-space pixel
size $\Delta x$ gives a maximum transverse spatial frequency
$k_{\perp,\mathrm{Nyq}}=1/(2\Delta x)$. Since
$k_\perp=k_0\sin\theta$ and $k_0=1/\lambda$, the corresponding Nyquist
angle is

\[
\theta_{\mathrm{Nyq}}
= \arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\Delta x}\right)
\approx 320~\mathrm{mrad}.
\]

Here we used $\lambda \approx 0.025\,\text{\AA}$ at 200~keV and
$\Delta x = 0.04\,\text{\AA}$. This angle is still far from the
evanescent boundary at $\theta=90^\circ$, where the square root in the ASM
kernel becomes singular. In the present benchmarks, AS-MS therefore has the
same per-slice FFT cost as F-MS and does not require substantially finer axial
or transverse sampling once the simulation already resolves the high-angle
features of interest.

\subsection{WP-MS contribution}

WP-MS makes a further correction by allowing the propagation kernel to depend on
the local electrostatic refractive index, so the wavelength changes inside each
slice. In the electron-optics setting, this gives
a differentiable one-way split-step method that retains FFT-based propagation
while incorporating a potential-dependent wavelength.

WP-MS is closest to the ODE reference in the Au~[100] benchmark, and the
residual AS-MS--WP-MS difference in the thick Au~[100] CBED calculation is
consistent with this additional local-wavelength correction. The refractive
index near the centre of a gold atom is approximately 1.07 in this simulation,
large enough that the correction can accumulate over tens of nanometres. Under
the high-energy conditions considered here, however, this effect is smaller
than the improvement obtained by replacing the Fresnel propagator with the
angular-spectrum propagator. WP-MS should therefore be seen as the more
complete one-way reference model in these tests, while AS-MS provides most of
the practical correction at essentially the same per-slice cost as F-MS. 


\subsection{Consequences and limits}

The silicon CBED calculation (Fig.~\ref{fig:si111_cbed_combined}) shows that the same propagator error is not limited to the most severe high-$Z$ case. Silicon scatters more weakly than gold, so the
CBED amplitude differences are smaller than in the thick Au~[100] calculation,
but the F-MS peak shifts still follow the angular dispersion error. These
high-angle peaks would often be damped by thermal diffuse scattering in a
room-temperature TEM experiment, particularly beyond about 200~mrad, and a
conventional image simulation can hide part of the error if the transfer
function or objective aperture suppresses the affected Fourier components.
CBED and ptychographic forward models use diffraction-plane intensities more
directly, so the same propagator error can become more visible.

In a ptychographic reconstruction, the forward model predicts detector
intensities for each probe position. If the propagator gives the wrong
high-angle amplitudes, peak positions, or accumulated phase through a thick
specimen, the optimization can compensate through the recovered potential.
Although ptychographic reconstruction for specimens as thick as the Au~[100]
CBED test is not yet routine, the calculation shows that the choice of
propagator can change the diffraction-plane amplitudes before other
experimental effects are added. For forward models that already use F-MS, most
of the wide-angle correction can therefore be recovered by replacing the
Fresnel kernel with the angular-spectrum kernel, while keeping the same
transmission step and per-slice FFT complexity.

The comparison here remains restricted to high-energy, elastic,
forward-scattering calculations. Lower accelerating voltages should be tested
separately because wide-angle propagation, potential-dependent wavelength
changes, and backscattering effects become more significant at lower energies
~\cite{ming2013pcms,WackerSchroeder2015,hillier_role_2017}. The WP-MS
implementation used here is one-way, but unlike the AS-MS or F-MS methods, 
can be formulated into a form that can include bidirectional propagation
~\cite{FertigBrenner2010,Fertig2011,WendeDrozellaHerkommer2023}. Adapting the bidirectional WP-MS to electron optics could then provide a practical alternative to the FC-MS method of Chen and Van Dyck~\cite{ChenVanDyck1997}. 

\section{Conclusion}

We adapted the optimized wave propagation method developed in scalar optics to
electron microscopy, giving a wave-propagation multislice (WP-MS) method that
tracks high-angle propagation and local wavelength changes within a
differentiable split-step framework. We then compared F-MS, AS-MS, and WP-MS in
three complementary settings: an Au~[100] ODE benchmark, a lower-$Z$ Si~[111]
CBED peak-position test, and a thick Au~[100] CBED amplitude test.

Across these tests, replacing the Fresnel kernel with the exact
angular-spectrum kernel accounts for most of the improvement over conventional
multislice. AS-MS keeps the same transmission step, FFT structure, and
per-slice FFT cost as F-MS, and under the conditions studied here it does not
require substantially finer sampling once the high-angle features are already
represented. For high-angle elastic forward simulations where F-MS is still
being used, AS-MS is therefore the most direct practical default.

WP-MS is the more complete one-way split-step model and is closest to the ODE
reference in the Au~[100] benchmark, but its additional accuracy over AS-MS is
modest under the high-energy conditions tested here. Its main role in this work
is to bridge the wave propagation method from scalar optics into electron
optics and to provide a differentiable local-wavelength extension of
angular-spectrum multislice. Future work should test the WP-MS methods performance at lower
accelerating voltages, against the bidirectional FC-MS method and inside ptychographic reconstructions where
systematic forward-model errors could bias the recovered potential.
