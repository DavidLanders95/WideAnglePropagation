\title{Wide-Angle Multislice for Electron Microscopy via the Wave Propagation Method}
\author{Author Name}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Multislice simulation is the standard method for modeling electron beam-sample interactions in transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
The traditional multislice algorithm is calculated using a split-step Fourier method that employs a paraxial (Fresnel) propagator, limiting its accuracy
to small scattering angles (typically $<7^\circ$) and ignoring the change of the electron wavelength inside the sample potential.
These limitations can be significant in scenarios involving high-angle scattering or thick, high-Z samples.
In this work, we present a comparison of three practical multislice propagators of increasing physical complexity:
F-MS, angular spectrum multislice, and wave propagation multislice (WP-MS), adapted from the Wave Propagation Method (WPM) in light optics, benchmarked against a forward ODE solver for the Klein-Gordon equation.
We write  Klein-Gordon, in it's Helmholtz form, adapt the WPM to electron microscopy via WP-MS and a differentiable refractive-index binning strategy,
and benchmark the methods against high-angle reference solutions in two settings: the Rother--Scheerschmidt Au~[100] beam-amplitude verification and convergent-beam diffraction in Au~[100].
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Image simulations are increasingly being used as
forward models within inverse reconstruction algorithms that recover
two-dimensional (2D) projected potentials or three-dimensional (3D) potentials
to answer materials-science questions about atomic specimens. This is in contrast
with their traditional use as a forward model for comparison with an experimental
image, in which a structure was proposed, an image was calculated
from that structure, and agreement with experiment was improved by trial and
error. In reconstruction workflows, the accuracy of the forward model is more
critical than ever because the model is not merely used for semi-quantitative
comparison, but is a central component of the reconstruction algorithm.

The standard tool for simulating electron propagation through a crystalline
specimen is the paraxial Fresnel multislice (F-MS) method, primarily due to its
simplicity and the computational efficiency provided by the fast Fourier
transform (FFT). In paraxial multislice, the specimen is divided into thin
slices perpendicular to the beam direction. Each slice applies a projected
transmission function that phase-shifts the wave, after which the wave is
propagated through free space to the next slice. F-MS is usually adequate for
the high-energy electrons, thin specimens, and low-$Z$ materials typical of
conventional TEM and STEM analysis. Discrepancies between simulation and
experiment are often dominated by other factors, such as thermal diffuse
scattering, detector response, or microscope aberration characterization.
However, for low incident electron energies, thick specimens, or high-$Z$
materials, F-MS can exhibit significant errors relative to more accurate
methods because of its paraxial approximation and omission of backscattering
~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009,Schmidt2016}.

The simplest improvement to F-MS is to replace the paraxial, parabolic Fresnel
propagator between slices with the exact spherical-wave propagator. To our
knowledge, \cite{2013} were the first to explicitly propose this
change in electron microscopy without expanding the propagator in a Taylor
series. Equation~7 of their work gives a
spherical-wave propagator that can be evaluated efficiently using the angular
spectrum method. We refer to this approach as angular spectrum multislice
(AS-MS), following terminology long established in light optics. They showed
visually significant differences between F-MS and AS-MS for specimens up to
20~nm thick at incident energies of 100~keV and below, particularly for
high-$Z$ materials at low energies. Although AS-MS is easy to implement and
requires almost no additional computational cost relative to F-MS, it does not
appear to have been widely adopted by the electron microscopy multislice
community.

Further improvements in accuracy, particularly the treatment of large-angle
scattering and backward-propagating components, incur significant computational
cost. A more accurate electron microscopy multislice algorithm is the
Chen--Van Dyck method, also known as fully corrected multislice (FCMS). Its
complexity arises because the full second-order wave equation contains a
square-root operator that mixes a transverse derivative term with the potential
and cannot be evaluated as a single Fourier-space propagator.

The Chen--Van Dyck method addresses this problem by expanding the square-root
operator in a Taylor series and retaining higher-order correction terms that
couple the potential to transverse derivatives. In this way the method accounts
more accurately for how the wave evolves within the potential of each slice,
rather than treating the slice only as a projected phase grating followed by
free-space propagation. This allows improved treatment of larger scattering
angles, low-energy electrons, thicker specimens, and high-$Z$ materials and,
in its more complete formulation, also gives access to backward-propagating
components.

5 years before the Chen-Van Dyck method was introduced in the field of scalar light 
optics, a solution to the identical mathematical problem of solving a Time Independent Wave Equation
to higher accuracy than the F-MS or AS-MS method had already been proposed, known as 
the Pad\'e Beam-Propagation Method (Pad\'e BPM)
~\cite{Hadley1992}. In light optics, the time-independent Helmholtz equation also
leads to a square-root propagation operator when it is factorized into forward-
and backward-propagating branches. Pad\'e beam-propagation methods approximate
this square-root operator using rational functions rather than the Taylor
polynomials proposed by Chen--Van Dyck. Because a Pad\'e approximant is a ratio
of polynomials, it can represent the square-root dispersion relation more
accurately over a wider angular range than a truncated Taylor series of the
same order. In practice, this often gives improved stability and accuracy for
large-angle propagation, allowing lower-order Pad\'e approximants to achieve
accuracy that would otherwise require higher-order Taylor corrections. In
either case, approximating the square-root operator remains computationally
expensive and incurs significant overhead relative to F-MS or AS-MS.

In electron optics, a still more accurate reference can be obtained by directly
integrating the full second-order time-independent wave equation as an ordinary
differential equation (ODE). Numerous authors have used such solutions as
references for assessing the accuracy of F-MS and the Chen--Van Dyck method.
Wacker and Schr\"oder provide a clear derivation of the ODE and
operator-approximation methods and benchmark them against conventional F-MS and
direct Runge--Kutta integration of the second-order Schr\"odinger equation.
Their results again showed that the F-MS method can introduce
substantial errors at low voltages, large specimen thicknesses, and high
scattering angles. Rother and Scheerschmidt approached the problem from a
relativistic perspective, using ODE solutions of the Dirac, Klein--Gordon, and
relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger wave equations and comparing them with
a paraxial multislice solution. Their work similarly indicates that the
dominant high-angle error arises from the parabolic propagation step used in
F-MS. These operator and ODE methods cannot exploit the FFT as directly as
split-step multislice, limiting their practical use as forward models within
inverse reconstruction algorithms.

The light-optics community has sought alternatives that retain the
computational efficiency of the FFT while avoiding the numerical difficulties
of evaluating Pad\'e BPM approximants and still providing a nonparaxial solution
to the time-independent wave equation. One such method is the Wave Propagation
Method (WPM), introduced by Brenner in 1993~\cite{Brenner1993}. In the WPM, the
medium is treated as locally homogeneous over small transverse regions, and a
corresponding homogeneous one-way propagator is applied for each representative
refractive index. The resulting fields are then recombined according to the
local refractive-index distribution. This retains the potential-dependent
wavelength within the propagation step without explicitly forming the full
dense square-root operator. Efficient variants of the WPM, including a recent
differentiable formulation, have been developed~\cite{Schmidt2016,DiffWPM2025}.
These methods approximate the nonparaxial wave
equation in Fourier space, enabling the use of the FFT while avoiding the need
to choose a Taylor-series cutoff, a noted drawback of the Chen--Van Dyck method
~\cite{2025ChenDyckPaper}. The WPM is therefore a promising starting point for a
practical wide-angle multislice method, but to our knowledge this adaptation has
not yet been systematically assessed in the electron microscopy context.

The aim of this work is therefore to evaluate high-angle propagation methods
that retain the FFT-based split-step structure that makes multislice practical,
using representative thick specimens at high electron energies, and demonstrate an alternative
high-angle propagator to the Chen Van-Dyck method (ignoring backscattering). In particular, we
compare AS-MS and WP-MS against both the traditional F-MS method and a forward-ODE
reference solution of the full second-order time-independent wave equation. We
quantify accuracy and computational cost and determine whether either method
can provide an efficient correction to paraxial multislice for representative
gold TEM and STEM simulations.

\section{Background Theory}
\label{sec:propagation_models}

\subsection{Wave equation and refractive index}
\label{sec:wave_equation_refractive_index}

We start from the scalar relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger equation for an
electron in an electrostatic potential,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro}
\nabla^2\psi+
\left(k_0^2-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2}V(\mathbf r)\right)\psi=0,
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ is the electron wavefunction, $V(\mathbf r)$ is the local
electrostatic potential energy, $m_0$ is the electron rest mass, $\gamma$ is the
Lorentz factor, and
\[
k_0 = \frac{\sqrt{E^2-m_0^2c^4}}{\hbar c}
\]
is the vacuum wavenumber. Equation~\eqref{eq:relativistic_schrodinger_intro} is
relativistically corrected because it keeps the relativistic electron momentum,
but neglects the quadratic term in the specimen potential in the full
relativistic energy--momentum relation. The same equation can be written in
Helmholtz form as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:helmholtz_intro}
\nabla^2\psi+k_0^2 n^2(\mathbf r)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
For the relativistically corrected Schr\"odinger equation this corresponds to
the refractive index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:index_schrodinger_intro}
n_\mathrm{S}^2(\mathbf r)
=
1-\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2k_0^2}V(\mathbf r).
\end{equation}

This identification of the electrostatic potential with an electron-optical
refractive index is due to~\cite{Glaser1933, Ehrenberg-Siday1949, Reimer1989}.
This notation is useful because the choice of scalar wave equation can be
expressed through the choice of $n(\mathbf r)$, separating approximations to the
wave equation from approximations to the potential. For example, retaining the
quadratic potential-energy term gives the time-independent Klein--Gordon
refractive index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:index_kg_intro}
n_\mathrm{KG}^2(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{(E-V(\mathbf r))^2-m_0^2c^4}{E^2-m_0^2c^4}.
\end{equation}
This is the refractive index used in the Klein--Gordon wave equation.

\subsection{Fresnel and angular-spectrum multislice}
\label{sec:fresnel_angular_spectrum}

To connect equation~\eqref{eq:helmholtz_intro} to conventional multislice, we
make the standard high-energy, or slowly varying envelope approximation used in
electron microscopy~\cite{kirkland2010advanced}. This approximation should be
distinguished from the separate linearisation of the refractive index, in which
the $V^2$ term in
equation~\eqref{eq:index_kg_intro} is neglected to obtain
equation~\eqref{eq:index_schrodinger_intro}. Writing the wavefunction as a
rapidly oscillating carrier multiplied by a slowly varying envelope,
\[
\psi(x,y,z)=e^{ik_0z}\tilde\psi(x,y,z),
\]
gives the parabolic wave equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:paraxial_helmholtz_intro}
\frac{\partial\tilde\psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{2k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\tilde\psi
+
\frac{ik_0}{2}\left[n^2(x,y,z)-1\right]\tilde\psi .
\end{equation}
The F-MS method is the split-step solution of
equation~\eqref{eq:paraxial_helmholtz_intro}: the specimen is applied as a thin
transmission function, and the wave is propagated between slices using the
Fresnel propagation kernel. Dropping the tilde for notational simplicity, one
slice can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fresnel_step}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
\approx
\exp\left[
\frac{ik_0}{2}\left(n^2(x,y)-1\right)\Delta z
\right]
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
\exp\left(-i\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\right].
\end{equation}
This form is fast because each slice requires only one FFT pair. Its main
propagation approximation is also clear: the free-space longitudinal wavevector
has been expanded as
\[
k_z
=
\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}
\simeq
k_0-\frac{k_\perp^2}{2k_0}.
\]
After removal of the carrier phase $e^{ik_0z}$, this expansion gives the
Fresnel kernel in equation~\eqref{eq:fresnel_step}. Conventional multislice is
therefore both a split-step approximation to the specimen and a small-angle
approximation to the longitudinal dispersion relation.

The angular spectrum method keeps the same split-step structure but replaces the
Fresnel kernel by the exact free-space dispersion relation. For the
carrier-removed wave this gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:asm_step}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
\approx
\exp\left[
\frac{ik_0}{2}\left(n^2(x,y)-1\right)\Delta z
\right]
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
\exp\left(i\left[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right]\Delta z\right)
\mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\right].
\end{equation}
Apart from the change in the Fourier-space phase factor, the algorithm is the
same as standard multislice and has the same $\mathcal O(P\log P)$ per-slice
cost, where $P$ is the number of transverse pixels. It is therefore the smallest
change one can make to F-MS in order to restore the exact
free-space dispersion relation. The approximation that remains is the split-step
treatment of the specimen: the potential is still applied as a thin transmission
function, while propagation between slices is still performed in vacuum.

\subsection{Classical Wave Propagation Method in scalar optics}
\label{sec:wpm_optics}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{method_explainer_generations/wpm}
    \caption{Schematic of the wave propagation multislice (WP-MS) adaptation of
    the Wave Propagation Method (WPM). The input wave is
    propagated using a bank of homogeneous angular-spectrum kernels, each
    evaluated at a different representative refractive index $n_m$. At each
    output pixel, the propagated field corresponding to the local refractive
    index is selected via soft-binned interpolation weights and recombined.}
    \label{fig:wpm_explainer}
\end{figure}

The Wave Propagation Method (WPM) addresses the remaining split-step
approximation in AS-MS by allowing the propagation kernel itself to depend on
the refractive index of the medium. The second-order
Helmholtz equation can be rearranged into an operator equation with a forward and
backward branch. Ignoring the backward branch, the forward branch may be written
as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}
\partial_z\psi
=
i\left[
\sqrt{k_0^2n^2(x,y,z)+\nabla_\perp^2}-k_0
\right]\psi .
\end{equation}
The central concern is that the square-root operator inside the wave equation
mixes the potential and the transverse derivative, and cannot be evaluated as a
single Fourier-space propagator. Pad\'e BPM and Chen--Van Dyck methods solve for
this operator using rational or series approximations to the square-root term,
which are expensive and cannot directly use the FFT.

Looking again at the AS-MS propagation kernel,
\[
\left[\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right]
\]
what is missing is a refractive-index term. We can reintroduce this term locally
by replacing $k_0$ with $k_0n(x,y)$, giving

\[
\left[\sqrt{k_0^2n^2(x,y)-k_\perp^2}-k_0\right].
\]

For a single refractive-index value, this kernel can be evaluated
straightforwardly with the angular spectrum method. The WPM simply extends the
angular spectrum method: we evaluate a bank of homogeneous angular-spectrum
propagators for each refractive index in the slice, and then retain the
propagated field corresponding to the local refractive index at each output
pixel.

This operation is obviously simplistic, because output pixels can receive a
propagated field that travelled from some pixels with the incorrect refractive
index. However, the approximation lives in the fact that those different
refractive indices are further away from the local refractive-index value of
that pixel, and therefore contribute less to the final result. In this manner,
the propagation step accounts for the local wavelength inside the specimen
without explicitly evaluating the full square-root operator. Schmidt
et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016} showed that the leading error from neglecting coupling
between neighbouring refractive-index regions affects amplitude rather than
phase; consequently, the WPM phase propagation is correct up to second order.
Although the approximation seems crude, this explains why the WPM provides an
accurate approximation to propagation through moderately varying inhomogeneous
media while remaining compatible with FFT-based computation.

Figure~\ref{fig:wpm_explainer} shows the same local-homogeneous idea in the
form used by our electron-microscopy adaptation below. In the classical WPM as
implemented by Schmidt, a mask is created to separate out each unique refractive index in a
slice, the angular spectrum kernel is calculated for each refractive index, and
then the mask is used to choose the correct angular spectrum solution from the
bank of all computed propagations.

In Brenner's original formulation~\cite{Brenner1993}, evaluating a different
propagation kernel at every output pixel is prohibitively expensive. For a
transverse grid with $P$ pixels, the direct mixed real/Fourier-space calculation
scales as
$\mathcal O(P^2)$. Schmidt et al.~\cite{Schmidt2016} made the method practical
for many optical simulations by grouping pixels with the same refractive
index. Only one homogeneous angular-spectrum propagation is then required for
each distinct index value. If a slice contains $M$ such values, the cost is
reduced to approximately
\[
\mathcal O(MP\log P),
\]
instead of a direct all-to-all evaluation.

This saving is large in light-optical simulations where a slice may contain only
a small number of unique refractive indices. It is less immediate in electron
microscopy, where the electrostatic potential varies continuously and almost
every pixel may have a different refractive index. Exact grouping therefore
gives little benefit unless the refractive-index field is deliberately
discretised. A second issue is that hard grouping introduces sharp masks, which
are inconvenient for gradient-based optimisation because the assignment of pixels
to refractive-index groups is not smoothly differentiable.

\subsection{Wave propagation multislice}
\label{sec:wp_ms}

We refer to our electron-microscopy adaptation of the WPM as wave propagation
multislice (WP-MS), to distinguish it from the classical WPM used in scalar
light optics. The change is not the underlying local-homogeneous idea, but how
that idea is made usable for multislice electron simulations. In electron
microscopy the refractive-index field is obtained from the electrostatic
potential, varies continuously across a slice, and must remain differentiable if
the propagator is to be used inside gradient-based reconstruction.

Following the differentiable WPM idea of~\cite{DiffWPM2025}, WP-MS therefore
uses a soft binning strategy instead of hard refractive-index masks.

\subsubsection{Differentiable refractive-index binning}
\label{sec:binning}

To make the computation of WP-MS tractable, and to avoid the naive
$O(N^{2D})$ calculation for a $D$-dimensional transverse grid, we adapt the WPM
algorithm presented in~\cite{DiffWPM2025}. This uses a refractive-index binning
strategy to limit the number of unique refractive-index values in a slice while
maintaining differentiability with respect to the refractive-index map
$n(x,y)$.

To implement this method, we first discretise the refractive-index range
$[n_{\mathrm{min}}, n_{\mathrm{max}}]$ of the current slice into
$N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ discrete reference values, denoted as
$\{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}}$.

In a typical multislice potential, many of the refractive-index values will be
very close to $1$ in the vacuum region, while the refractive-index values inside
an atom will concentrate in a narrow range slightly above $1$. Since there are
likely to be many more refractive-index values near the vacuum region, we employ
a quadratic distribution for the reference indices rather than using linearly
spaced bins, in order to allocate more bins near $n=1$. The reference indices
are distributed according to a quadratic function determined by the parameter
$\gamma$, with $\gamma=2.0$ for quadratic spacing:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bin_spacing}
n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}
=
n_{\mathrm{min}}
+
(n_{\mathrm{max}} - n_{\mathrm{min}})
\left( \frac{k-1}{N_{\mathrm{bins}}-1} \right)^\gamma,
\quad
k=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{bins}},
\end{equation}

which allows for denser binning near the vacuum refractive index when $\gamma > 1$.

Next, for a propagation step $\Delta z$, we compute a bank of propagated fields
$u_k(x,y)$. Each field $u_k$ corresponds to the diffraction of the input field
$u_{\mathrm{in}}(x,y)$ assuming a homogeneous medium with refractive index
$n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}$. This is performed efficiently using batch FFT
operations with the angular spectrum kernel:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_binned_fields}
u_k(x,y)
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
\left[
\mathcal{F}\{u_{\mathrm{in}}\}
\exp\left(
i \Delta z
\left[\sqrt{(k_0 n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)})^2-k_x^2-k_y^2}-k_0\right]
\right)
\right].
\end{equation}

To evaluate the field at a specific spatial coordinate $(x,y)$ where the local
refractive index is $n(x,y)$, we identify the two bounding reference bins such
that $n_L \le n(x,y) < n_R$, where
$n_L,n_R \in \{n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(k)}\}$. We adopt a differentiable interpolation
scheme by defining a local interpolation weight

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_raw_weight}
w_{\mathrm{raw}} = \frac{n(x,y) - n_L}{n_R - n_L}, \quad w_{\mathrm{raw}} \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}

To ensure smooth gradients and minimise discretisation artifacts, we map this
linear weight through a smoothstep activation function $S(w)$, akin to the
spatial partitioning technique demonstrated by~\cite{BarreBrunel} via a cubic
Hermite interpolation function:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_smoothstep}
w = S(w_{\mathrm{raw}}) = 3w_{\mathrm{raw}}^2 - 2w_{\mathrm{raw}}^3
\end{equation}
While linear interpolation would satisfy the requirement for differentiability,
it could result in discontinuities in the gradient at the bin boundaries in a
gradient-based optimisation scheme.

The final field at the next step $u(x,y,z+\Delta z)$ is computed as the
weighted superposition of the fields propagated at the bounding indices:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_two_bin_interpolation}
u(x,y) = (1 - w) \cdot u_L(x,y) + w \cdot u_R(x,y)
\end{equation}

Equivalently, with only the two neighbouring weights non-zero at each pixel, the
WP-MS update can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wpm_bank}
\psi(x,y,z+\Delta z)
\approx
\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{bins}}} w_m(x,y)
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
\exp\left(
i\left[
\sqrt{k_0^2\left(n_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(m)}\right)^2-k_\perp^2}
-k_0
\right]\Delta z
\right)
\mathcal F\{\psi(x,y,z)\}
\right].
\end{equation}
This gives a differentiable approximation to the one-way medium propagator in
equation~\eqref{eq:one_way_square_root_intro}. When the slice is homogeneous,
only one refractive-index bin is needed and WP-MS reduces to the angular
spectrum method. For an inhomogeneous specimen, the method is more expensive
than AS-MS because many FFT-based propagations are required per slice, but it
accounts for the local wavelength inside the specimen rather than propagating
every slice with the vacuum wavelength.

This formulation ensures that the propagation model is fully differentiable
with respect to the refractive-index map $n(x,y)$, enabling inverse
reconstruction of potentials via automatic differentiation. It also means that
only a limited number of FFTs, equal to $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$, are required per
propagation step, significantly reducing the computational cost compared to a
naive WPM implementation. The accuracy of WP-MS
depends on the number of bins $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ chosen, and will change on a
case-by-case basis depending on the refractive-index distribution of the sample.
Figure~\ref{fig:wpm_binning_diagnostics} shows a representative diagnostic of
the binning procedure. A practical way to mitigate this potential source of
error is to perform a test simulation and double the number of bins until no
difference is observed in the output wavefunction.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{wpm_binning_diagnostics}
    \caption{Diagnostics of the differentiable WP-MS binning strategy for a representative
    MoS$_2$ slice. Top row: discretized refractive-index map (bin lower bound $n_L$),
    smooth interpolation weights $w$, and histogram of the true refractive-index

    cross-section comparing the original and fitted refractive-index profiles.}
    \label{fig:wpm_binning_diagnostics}

In general, WP-MS will need a GPU for efficient computation, and we have found
that $N_{\mathrm{bins}}$ between 64 and 512 are sufficient for the simulations
presented in this work. This is a marked improvement over the naive case, as it
enables WP-MS to simulate many slices with larger grid sizes
($512 \times 512$ or $1024 \times 1024$ pixels) in a reasonable time frame
(seconds to minutes), provided the refractive-index binning strategy is capable
of correctly capturing the refractive-index distribution of the sample.

\subsection{Forward ODE reference}
\label{sec:forward_ode_reference}

We use a direct ODE solution of the second-order Klein--Gordon Helmholtz
equation as the reference calculation. This is a standard way to solve a wave
equation numerically: the transverse coordinates are discretised, the
second-order equation is rewritten as a first-order system, and the resulting
large system of ordinary differential equations is integrated along $z$.
Substituting $n_\mathrm{KG}$ into the Helmholtz equation gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_second_order}
\partial_z^2\psi
=
-\left(\nabla_\perp^2+k_0^2n_\mathrm{KG}^2(x,y,z)\right)\psi .
\end{equation}
Introducing $\phi=\partial_z\psi$ gives the equivalent first-order system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_state}
\frac{d}{dz}
\begin{pmatrix}
\psi\\
\phi
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\phi\\
-\left(\nabla_\perp^2+k_0^2n_\mathrm{KG}^2(x,y,z)\right)\psi
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We integrate this system with an adaptive high-order Runge--Kutta method. The
transverse Laplacian is evaluated spectrally using FFTs, while the potential
term is applied by pointwise multiplication in real space.

The calculation is initialised on the forward-propagating vacuum branch. The
entrance wave $\psi(z=0)$ is supplemented by the longitudinal derivative
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kg_phi0}
\phi(z=0)
=
\mathcal F^{-1}
\left[
i\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}\,
\mathcal F\{\psi(z=0)\}
\right],
\end{equation}
which selects the positive-$k_z$ branch of the incident field in vacuum. We
therefore refer to this as the forward ODE reference. The method is much more
expensive than the split-step propagation methods, and only serves to provide a
direct numerical reference for the forward-scattering calculations considered
here.

\subsection{Relationship between the methods}
\label{sec:method_relationship}

The four methods can therefore be read as successive changes to the same
underlying Helmholtz problem, with a chosen refractive index representing either
the Klein--Gordon or the relativistically corrected version of the
time-independent wave equation. F-MS uses a split-step specimen approximation
together with the paraxial free-space propagator. AS-MS keeps the
split-step approximation but restores the exact free-space propagator. WP-MS
approximates the one-way propagation operator inside the medium by local
homogeneous propagation. The forward ODE keeps the second-order
Klein--Gordon Helmholtz equation and evolves the transverse Laplacian and
specimen potential together; it is used as the reference calculation.

\section{Results}

We present two numerical experiments that isolate the high-angle behaviour most relevant to this
work. Both compare F-MS (FMS), angular spectrum multislice (ASMS), and wave
propagation multislice (WP-MS) against a converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon
equation. The first reproduces the Au~[100] beam-amplitude benchmark of Rother and
Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}; the second tests a convergent Au~[110] probe over a
wide range of semi-angles.

\subsection{Verification against Rother \& Scheerschmidt (2009)}
\label{sec:verification}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
    \caption{Real-space exit-wave intensity and phase for the Au~[100] verification case. The rightmost column shows the HRTEM image intensity after applying a representative aberration-corrected 300~keV transfer function with residual $C_s=10~\mu\mathrm{m}$, a non-inverting defocus of $+6.1$~nm, a 12~mrad objective aperture, and 2~nm focal spread. The defocus is selected so that the phase CTF remains positive throughout the transmitted band, avoiding contrast inversion. Color scales are shared across methods within each column, and image-plane NRMSE values are relative to the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes}
\end{figure}

To verify our implementation, and assess which propagation methods retain accuracy at high angles in thick samples with a sample of a high atomic number, we recreated the simulation presented in figure~3 of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, which propagates a plane wave input through a thick gold [100] crystal at 300~keV. The ground truth is the converged ODE solution of the full second-order Klein-Gordon equation. In this example, periodic boundary conditions are implicit, due to each method using a Fourier transform solution, and as a result the crystal is infinitely wide. Since we wished to explore the accuracy of each method in thicker objects, we extended the simulation as presented by Rother and Scheerschmidt from 25 unit cells (~10nm thickness) to 100 unit cells (~40nm thickness) It is clear in \ref{fig:au_exit_wave_amplitudes}, which displays the exit-wave intensity, phase, and transfer-function image intensity after propagation through 100 unit cells of the gold crystal, that the Fresnel Propagator method is qualitatively different from the other three methods. With the non-inverting phase CTF applied, the difference remains: the image-plane NRMSE relative to the KG ODE reference is $1.88\times10^{-2}$ for WP-MS, $2.15\times10^{-2}$ for angular spectrum multislice, and $1.50\times10^{-1}$ for Fresnel multislice. The angular spectrum multislice, WP-MS, and KG ODE solutions all closely track each other, with the the WP-MS solution appearing to track more accurately the amplitude of the pixels in the ground truth solution compared to the angular spectrum method. However, as noted by Rother and Scheerschmidt, the images can hardly be compared quantitatively by eye: as a result, we have repeated their experiment in figure-3 by plotting the normalised amplitude of the fourier transform of the exit wave after each slice for the two selected pixels - an on axis [0, 0] pixel in the centre of the image, and a high-angle [0, 28] pixel at ~135mrad from 0 to 25, and from 0 to 100 unit cells.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Au_pixel_amplitudes}
    \caption{Pixel-amplitude comparison for the Au~[100] verification case. The central and high-angle pixels are compared across F-MS, angular spectrum multislice, wave propagation multislice, and the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:au_pixel_amplitudes}
\end{figure}

For the central pixel [0, 0], all methods agree closely up to ~10nm thickness or 25 unit cells. We extented this simulation out to 100 unit cells or 40 nm thickness, and found that past 10nm the error between the fresnel propagator and all other methods begins to increase even for the on-axis [0,0] pixel. Past 15nm of thickness, there has been enough propagation through the crystal that higher angle diffracted beams can make their way back towards the central pixel and modify it's value. The F-MS method is not capable of tracking these beams, and thus it's accuracy suffers as a result. For the high-angle pixel [0, 28] at 135~mrad, the F-MS solution diverges from the ground truth after approximately 10 unit cells or 4nm, consistent again with the findings of \cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009} who attributed the discrepancy to the linearization of the square-root operator. Interestingly, the AS-MS method, which requires only a small implementation change to the standard F-MS method, tracks the ground truth much more closely than the F-MS solution, and is comparable to the WP-MS solution which is significantly more expensive to implement and compute. It is only at a very large thickness of ~35 nm in the off axis pixel that it becomes clear the angular spectrum is starting to deviate from the ground truth. The WP-MS solution tracks the ground truth more closely than the angular spectrum method, but the difference is small and only becomes apparent at very large thicknesses. At least in this case, the extra computational effort of WP-MS does not appear to provide a significant accuracy improvement over the AS-MS.

\subsection{Convergent-probe Au CBED}
\label{sec:convergence_angle}


Next, we tested each method's accuracy as a function of probe convergence semi-angle for a convergent-beam electron diffraction (CBED) geometry, on a 3 times enlarged version of the Au~[100] unit cell in x and y compared to the unit cell used in the previous section up to a thickness of ~100 nm. In order to ensure accurate tracking of the wave for a large convergence angle, we increased the sampling to 256 x 256 over the enlarged unit cell. The probe is focused on the entrance surface of the specimen, so the initial wavefunction in amplitude is an airy function without any aberrations. The input wave is then propagated through the specimen using each method and compared to the KG ODE ground truth. The CBED diffraction pattern is calculated at the exit surface by taking the fourier transform of the exit wave and compared across methods.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps}
    \caption{CBED exit-wave amplitude comparison in log scale for the Au~[100] convergence-angle study. The maps show the final diffraction patterns produced by F-MS, angular spectrum multislice, wave propagation multislice, and the Klein--Gordon ODE reference.}
    \label{fig:cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps}

A series of convergent probes including the following semi-angles - 25, 50, 100 mrad were propagated through 
using 128 slices per unit cell for each method. Figure~\ref{fig:cbed_combined_amplitude_log_error_maps} again confirms that both WP-MS and the angular spectrum method are capable of tracking the high-angle scattering more accurately than the F-MS method. Visually the final CBED pattern of the F-MS solution is noticeably worse than the WP-MS or AS-MS. WP-MS for all three convergence angles at the end of the propagation routine shows the smallest error in the CBED amplitude - staying on average at 3.5\% mean error compared to the ground truth ODE solution. The WP-MS solution tracks the ground truth more closely than the AS-MS method, but again the difference is small and only becomes apparent at very large thicknesses. Again, in this more extreme case, the extra computational effort of WP-MS does not appear to provide a significant accuracy improvement over the AS-MS method.

    \safeincludegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{cbed_error_vs_thickness}
    \caption{Relative CBED error as a function of specimen thickness for the Au~[100] convergence-angle study. The error is reported against the Klein--Gordon ODE reference at the end of each unit cell for the tested probe semi-angles.}

In Figure~\ref{fig:cbed_error_vs_thickness} we visualise the root mean square (RMSE) error of the CBED intensity against the KG ODE reference as a function of object thickness, where we see that after a thickness of approximately 10 nm in all cases, there is an order of magnitude error difference in the RMSE of the F-MS method compared to WP-MS and AS-MS. The AS-MS method does become less accurate as the thickness increases, but still remains closer to the accuracy of WP-MS compared to the F-MS method. WP-MS tracks the ground truth more closely than the angular spectrum method and at least up to these thicknesses, maintains a consistent relative error. Repeatedly, in these cases for thick samples, there does not appear to be a compelling reason to use WP-MS over the AS-MS method, given the small accuracy improvement and the significantly higher computational cost of WP-MS. Especially given the fact that the AS-MS method is a simple modification to the standard F-MS method, and can be implemented with no additional computational cost, it appears to be the best choice for high-angle scattering simulations in thick samples with high atomic $Z$ numbers in TEM. 

\section{Sampling Requirements}
\label{sec:sampling_requirements}

When replacing the Fresnel kernel with the angular spectrum kernel, the main
concern is a question of sampling. Are the typical existing FFT grid and slice thicknesses
still fine enough to represent the propagator? For F-MS this is
usually straightforward, because the kernel
$\exp(-i k_\perp^2 \Delta z / 2k_0)$ is smooth for all $k_\perp$. The angular
spectrum kernel $\exp(i\Delta z \sqrt{k_0^2 - k_\perp^2})$ is slightly more
delicate: its phase changes rapidly as $k_\perp$ approaches $k_0$, and for
$k_\perp > k_0$ the modes become evanescent. As a result, the usual
phase-sampling condition gives a slightly tighter slice-thickness bound for ASM
than for Fresnel. This factor comes from comparing how quickly the propagator
phase changes across transverse-frequency space. For one propagation step, the
Fresnel and ASM phases are

\[
\phi_{\mathrm{F}}(k_\perp) = -\frac{\Delta z}{2k_0}k_\perp^2,
\qquad
\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}(k_\perp) = \Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}.
\]

Their phase gradients are therefore

\[
\left|\frac{d\phi_{\mathrm{F}}}{dk_\perp}\right|
= \Delta z\frac{k_\perp}{k_0},
\qquad
\left|\frac{d\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}}{dk_\perp}\right|
= \Delta z\frac{k_\perp}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}.
\]

So the ASM phase varies faster by the ratio

\[
\frac{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{ASM}}/dk_\perp\right|}{\left|d\phi_{\mathrm{F}}/dk_\perp\right|}
= \frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2}}.
\]

Evaluating this at the largest represented transverse wavevector
$k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}$ gives the slightly tighter ASM slice-thickness bound,
by the factor

\[
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2 - k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}^2}}.
\]

In conventional TEM this extra restriction is usually negligible. For the
200~keV case discussed here, the numbers are:

\[
\lambda \approx 0.025\,\text{\AA},
\qquad
k_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \approx \frac{2\pi}{0.025} \approx 250\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

For a 200~mrad probe, the largest transverse wavevector is

\[
k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}} = k_0 \sin\alpha
\approx 250\sin(0.2)
\approx 50\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

So the relevant ratio is

\[
\frac{k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}}{k_0} \approx \frac{50}{250} \approx 0.2.
\]

The extra ASM sampling penalty is therefore

\[
\frac{k_0}{\sqrt{k_0^2-k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}^2}}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(k_{\perp,\mathrm{max}}/k_0)^2}}
\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.2^2}}
\approx 1.02,
\]

which means the slice-thickness bound is tightened by only about 2\%.

The same conclusion holds for the transverse grid. If the real-space pixel size
is $\Delta x = 0.04\,\text{\AA}$, then the Nyquist spatial frequency is

\[
f_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{1}{2\Delta x} = \frac{1}{0.08} = 12.5\,\text{\AA}^{-1},
\]

so the corresponding transverse wavevector is

\[
k_{\perp,\mathrm{Nyq}} = 2\pi f_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{\pi}{\Delta x}
\approx \frac{\pi}{0.04}
\approx 78.5\,\text{\AA}^{-1}.
\]

This gives a Nyquist scattering angle of

\[
\theta_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{k_{\perp,\mathrm{Nyq}}}{k_0}\right)
\approx \arcsin\!\left(\frac{78.5}{250}\right)
\approx 0.32\,\text{rad}
\approx 320\,\text{mrad}.
\]

This is still well below the singular point at $k_\perp = k_0$. The
transmission factor $\exp(i\sigma V_\mathrm{proj})$ places the same sampling
demands in Fresnel and ASM, since it depends only on the projected potential.
In practice, this means that if a grid is already adequate for high-quality
F-MS in TEM, it is usually also adequate for angular spectrum
multislice. Extra ASM-specific sampling only becomes important in regimes much
closer to $k_\perp = k_0$, such as extreme near-field or soft-X-ray problems
rather than the electron-microscopy cases studied here.

\section{Conclusion}

We have extended the optimised Wave Propagation Method developed in light optics to enable
its use in electron microscopy. The resulting approach, which we call Wave
Propagation Multislice (WP-MS), allows high-angle scattering and local
wavelength changes to be tracked through a slice. WP-MS provides an alternative
way of treating the one-way square-root propagation operator considered by the
Chen--Van Dyck method, but avoids the need to choose a Taylor-expansion cutoff value, and can
make use of the Fast Fourier Transform to accelerate the calculation. As a forward
model for inverse reconstruction methods in the future, our adjustments to the 
algorithm also make it fully differentiable in the context of electron microscopy simulations. 

We then used this reference implementation to compare three split-step multislice propagators of
increasing physical complexity---F-MS, AS-MS, and the WP-MS---against
a direct forward-ODE solution of the full second-order Klein--Gordon equation.
The comparisons include the Au~[100] beam-amplitude verification of Rother and
Scheerschmidt~\cite{RotherScheerschmidt2009}, extended here from 25 to 100 unit
cells, and convergent-beam diffraction in Au~[100] at probe semi-angles up to
100~mrad and specimen thicknesses up to roughly 100~nm. The forward ODE makes
neither a split-step nor a paraxial approximation, allowing us to attribute the
observed discrepancies to the choice of propagator.

Across both tests, replacing the Fresnel kernel with the exact angular-spectrum
kernel accounts for most of the improvement over conventional multislice. In a gold [100] sample
the F-MS deviates from all other methods after
only $\sim$4~nm in a 135 mrad beam. AS-MS and the WP-MS instead follow the ODE reference closely out
to 40~nm and differ appreciably only at the largest thicknesses. The
convergent-probe CBED study again confirms this result: beyond $\sim$10~nm, the
error of F-MS is roughly an order of magnitude larger than that of AS-MS and
the WPM across the 25, 50, and 100~mrad probes. The WPM is consistently the
most accurate split-step method, with an average CBED-amplitude error near
3.5\%, but its accuracy advantage over AS-MS is small in absolute terms and
comes at a substantially higher computational cost. This results are inline with those presented 
by Rother and Scheerschmidt, and \cite{2013ChenPaper} already, but extended to thicker samples.

Taken together, these results indicate again that the dominant missing physics in
F-MS for the high-energy, thick, high-$Z$ cases studied here is non-paraxial
free-space dispersion, rather than the local, potential-dependent wavelength
change that the WP-MS additionally captures. These results provide a further
reminder to the community: AS-MS improves on traditional F-MS for almost no
extra implementation or computational effort. For comparable simulations of
moderately thick specimens containing high-$Z$ atoms, we recommend the use of
AS-MS over F-MS. In practice, an existing F-MS implementation can recover most
of the available wide-angle accuracy by replacing
$\exp(-i k_\perp^2 \Delta z / 2k_0)$ with
$\exp(i\Delta z\sqrt{k_0^2-k_\perp^2})$, without changing the FFT grid, slice
thickness, transmission step, or per-slice computational complexity.
Section~\ref{sec:sampling_requirements} further shows that the
angular-spectrum kernel imposes only a few-percent tighter sampling bound under
the TEM conditions considered here.

Another outcome of this work has been to make explicit that the time-independent
Schr\"odinger equation used in multislice can be written as the same scalar
Helmholtz equation used in light optics, with the specimen potential entering
as an electrostatic refractive-index term. This equivalence means that, 
for a monoenergetic incident beam and within the
same scalar propagation assumptions, electron microscopy can borrow
developments from scalar light optics rather than treating them as a separate
formalism.

The present benchmarks are restricted to forward scattering in high-energy
gold specimens. The balance between AS-MS and the WP-MS may change at lower
accelerating voltages, in still thicker high-$Z$ specimens, or in other regimes
where the local wavelength differs more strongly from its vacuum value. Future
work will implement features of the Bidirectional Wave Propagation Method from
light optics, enabling the WP-MS to calculate backscattered waves. In turn, this
would enable us to benchmark the method against the Chen--Van Dyck method and
compare their accuracy and speed in low-energy sample simulations. The models  
presented here may become especially important as a forward model for low-energy ptychographic
reconstruction problems,  where high-angle and multiple-scattering effects can 
have a large effect on the simulated transmitted beam \cite{2013paper}
