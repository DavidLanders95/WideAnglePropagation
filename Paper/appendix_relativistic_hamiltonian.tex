\section{Relativistic Hamiltonian, Refractive Index, and Corrected Schr\"odinger Form}
\label{app:relativistic_hamiltonian}


In order to model the propagation of electron in matter one has to use a quantum mechanical description of the interaction between the imaging electrons and the atoms of the specimen and the electrons are thus to be described by a complex wave fucntion $\Phi(\mathbf r)$ that may be calculated at any position in the microscope.
Of course, in principe one should start with the Dirac Equation to describe the propagation of electrons, but it has been shown that the interaction can be described by a 'time-independent relatistically corrected' Schrodinger equation with sufficient accuracy (Fujiwara et Lubkt ), where the energy of the electron beam is not only the kinect energy, but the relavistic one and its mass is also the relativistic one. Below, we are going to derive again these results starting from a spin spin-independent relativistic Hamiltonian $\hat H$ of a charged particle  $q_e$ in an
electromagnetic field.

Following ~\cite{jackson1999classical} this hamiltonian has the form : 
\begin{equation}
\hat H
=
\sqrt{
c^2\left(\hat{\mathbf p}-q_e\mathbf A/c\right)^2
+
m_0^2c^4
}
+
q_e\Phi(\mathbf r),
\label{eq:app_minimal_coupling_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf A$ and $\Phi(\mathbf r)$ are respecively  the magnetic vector and electrostatic potentials, $m_0$ is the electron rest
mass, and $\hat{\mathbf p}=-i\hbar\nabla$ is the momentum operator. In the
free-electron--light problem, the interaction is mainly carried by
$\mathbf A$. In elastic electron diffraction by a crystal, we neglect
$\mathbf A$ and retain the scalar electrostatic potential. Equation
\eqref{eq:app_minimal_coupling_hamiltonian} then reduces to
\begin{equation}
\hat H
=
\sqrt{c^2\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}+m_0^2c^4}
+
V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r).
\label{eq:app_relativistic_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}

where now  $ E_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r) $ is the electron potential energy (measured for instance in eV or J) of an electron in the field  $V(\mathbf r)$ ( measured in volts) and $q_e=-e$ is the electron charge. Here $e>0$ as in Reimer and Kirkland.

\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
=
q_e\Phi(\mathbf r)
=
-e\Phi(\mathbf r),
\label{eq:app_electron_potential_energy}
\end{equation}




For the incident beam in vacuum, the conserved total relativistic energy is
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
mc^2
=
\gamma m_0c^2
=
\sqrt{c^2p_0^2+m_0^2c^4},
\qquad
p_0
=
hk_0
=
\frac{h}{\lambda},
\label{eq:app_incident_energy_momentum}
\end{equation}
where $m=\gamma m_0$ is the relativistic mass, $p_0$ is the vacuum
relativistic momentum, and $k_0=1/\lambda$ is the spatial frequency convention
used in the present paper. This is the same energy--momentum relation as
Fujiwara's Eq.~(2.16)~\cite{fujiwara1961relativistic}; Fujiwara denotes the
total energy by $E$ and uses the angular, or optical, convention
$K=2\pi/\lambda$ instead of $k_0=1/\lambda$. In the following we consider
elastic scattering, so the incident electron energy is conserved during its
interaction with the specimen.

A stationary state satisfies
\begin{equation}
\hat H\psi
=
E_{\mathrm{tot}}\psi,
\label{eq:app_stationary_relativistic_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:app_stationary_relativistic_hamiltonian} may be
rearranged as
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{c^2\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}+m_0^2c^4}\,\psi
=
\left[
E_{\mathrm{tot}}-V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]\psi .
\label{eq:app_square_root_rearranged}
\end{equation}
Squaring both sides gives the scalar Klein--Gordon-type equation
\begin{equation}
\left[
c^2\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}+m_0^2c^4
\right]\psi
=
\left[
E_{\mathrm{tot}}-V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]^2\psi .
\label{eq:app_kg_before_linearisation}
\end{equation}

For electron diffraction, the electrostatic potential energy is small compared
with $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Keeping terms only to first order in
$V_{\mathrm{pot}}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\left[
E_{\mathrm{tot}}-V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]^2
\simeq
E_{\mathrm{tot}}^2
-
2E_{\mathrm{tot}}V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r).
\label{eq:app_linearised_potential_energy}
\end{equation}
Using $\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}=-\hbar^2\nabla^2$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)
+
\left[
\frac{p_0^2}{\hbar^2}
-
\frac{2E_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\hbar^2c^2}
V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]\psi(\mathbf r)
=0,
\label{eq:app_linearised_kg_wave_equation}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_incident_energy_momentum}, the coefficient of the
potential term can be written as $\gamma m_0$ and
$p_0/\hbar=2\pi k_0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:app_linearised_kg_wave_equation}
therefore becomes
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)
+
\left[
(2\pi k_0)^2
-
\frac{2\gamma m_0}{\hbar^2}
V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]\psi(\mathbf r)
=0.
\label{eq:app_corrected_schrodinger_k0}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Corrected Schr\"odinger Form and the TEM Interaction Constant}
\label{app:corrected_schrodinger_sigma}

Writing the electron potential energy as
$V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)=-eU(\mathbf r)$, where $U$ is the specimen
electrostatic potential in volts and $e>0$, and introducing the usual TEM
interaction constant
\begin{equation}
\sigma
=
\frac{2\pi\gamma m_0 e}{h^2k_0}
=
\frac{2\pi m_{\mathrm r}e\lambda}{h^2},
\qquad
m_{\mathrm r}
=
\gamma m_0,
\label{eq:app_sigma_definition}
\end{equation}
the potential term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_corrected_schrodinger_k0} becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{2\gamma m_0e}{\hbar^2}U(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{2m_{\mathrm r}e}{\hbar^2}U(\mathbf r)
=
4\pi k_0\sigma U(\mathbf r).
\label{eq:app_potential_term_sigma}
\end{equation}
Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:app_corrected_schrodinger_k0} can be written as
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)
+
\left[
(2\pi k_0)^2
+
4\pi k_0\sigma U(\mathbf r)
\right]\psi(\mathbf r)
=
0.
\label{eq:app_corrected_schrodinger_sigma}
\end{equation}
This is the same corrected Schr\"odinger form used in TEM multislice theory;
the relativistic correction enters the interaction strength through
$m_{\mathrm r}=\gamma m_0$.

The same interaction constant appears after factoring out the fast
carrier wave as
\begin{equation}
\psi(\mathbf r)
=
\exp(2\pi i k_0z)\tilde\psi(\mathbf r),
\label{eq:app_carrier_wave}
\end{equation}
and neglecting $\partial^2\tilde\psi/\partial z^2$ gives the usual paraxial
form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\tilde\psi}{\partial z}
=
\frac{i}{4\pi k_0}\nabla_\perp^2\tilde\psi
+
i\sigma U(\mathbf r)\tilde\psi,
\label{eq:app_paraxial_sigma}
\end{equation}
For a thin slice of projected electrostatic potential
\begin{equation}
U_{\mathrm p}(x,y)
=
\int_{\mathrm{slice}} U(x,y,z)\,dz,
\label{eq:app_projected_potential}
\end{equation}
the corresponding phase grating is
\begin{equation}
t(x,y)
=
\exp\left[
i\sigma U_{\mathrm p}(x,y)
\right].
\label{eq:app_transmission_function_sigma}
\end{equation}
This is the common TEM notation.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon Refractive Index}
\label{app:kg_refractive_index}

The same Hamiltonian also gives the Klein--Gordon refractive index used in the
main text. Starting from the unlinearised scalar equation
\eqref{eq:app_kg_before_linearisation} and using
$\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}=-\hbar^2\nabla^2$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)
+
\frac{
\left[
E_{\mathrm{tot}}-V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]^2
-
m_0^2c^4
}
{\hbar^2c^2}
\psi(\mathbf r)
=
0.
\label{eq:app_unlinearised_kg_wave_equation}
\end{equation}
Using the vacuum relation between $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$ and $p_0$, together with
$p_0=2\pi\hbar k_0$, this can be written in Helmholtz form as
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r)
+
(2\pi k_0)^2 n_{\mathrm{KG}}^2(\mathbf r)\psi(\mathbf r)
=
0,
\label{eq:app_kg_helmholtz_form}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
n_{\mathrm{KG}}^2(\mathbf r)
=
\frac{
\left[
E_{\mathrm{tot}}-V_{\mathrm{pot}}(\mathbf r)
\right]^2
-
m_0^2c^4
}
{
E_{\mathrm{tot}}^2-m_0^2c^4
}.
\label{eq:app_kg_refractive_index_from_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $n_{\mathrm{KG}}(\mathbf r)$ is the ratio between the local
relativistic momentum and the vacuum momentum. This connects the modern
Klein--Gordon form to the older electron-optical refractive-index idea of
Glaser and of Ehrenberg and Siday, who related the index to the electron
generalized momentum and to the de Broglie wavelength
\cite{Glaser1933,Ehrenberg-Siday1949}.
